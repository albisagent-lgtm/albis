Deepfake technology is now weaponised simultaneously in armed conflict, democratic elections, and medical systems. In the past week, Iran deployed AI-generated propaganda videos targeting President Trump, US midterm campaigns began using deepfake political ads, and a major study revealed fake X-rays that fool radiologists. The synthetic media threat has gone from theoretical to operational across every domain that matters.

## Iran's AI propaganda machine

Iran's information war runs alongside its military one. Since the [US-Iran conflict](/topics/iran-war) began a month ago, Tehran's messaging apparatus has turned President Trump into its primary target through a sprawling digital campaign.

The content ranges from absurd to sophisticated. One AI-generated video depicts a Lego-style multiverse where Iranian soldiers battle miniature versions of Trump and Netanyahu. Another shows a Trump-like Teletubby character in the Oval Office playing with toy fighter jets. State media officials deliver English-language taunts using Trump's own catchphrases.

This isn't improvised. Researchers at Clemson University's Media Forensics Hub describe it as a sophisticated information operation aided by Russia and China. Iran's state media outlets and covert operatives produce a steady torrent of propaganda, overstated narratives, and outright [disinformation](/topics/disinformation) using generative AI tools.

One fabricated video this week showed a missile striking Liberty Island in New York Harbor. It was debunked. But not before reaching millions across X, Bluesky, Facebook, Instagram, and TikTok.

"They're winning the propaganda war," said Darren Linvill, a director of Clemson's Media Forensics Hub. "They were prepared for it more than the administration, because they'd been preparing for this entire conflict for 50 years."

## Deepfakes enter US midterm campaigns

The same technology is reshaping American democracy. Reuters reports that some campaigns already deploy deepfake advertisements ahead of November's 2026 midterm elections.

These aren't crude fakes. AI tools have improved at what researchers call "a breakneck pace." The result: realistic video content that's increasingly difficult to distinguish from genuine footage.

The Governance and Responsible AI Lab at Purdue University tracked the acceleration. Since the start of 2025, they catalogued more than 1,000 English-language social media posts featuring fake images or videos of political figures. In the previous eight years combined, they recorded 1,344 such incidents. The rate nearly doubled in just over a year.

"We are blending the lines between political cartoons and reality," said Daniel Schiff, co-director of the lab. "A lot of people feel like these images or videos, or the stories they convey, feel true."

That last point matters. The Guardian reports that political deepfakes exert influence even when viewers know they aren't real. The emotional impression lingers after the factual correction fades.

## The fake influencer pipeline

Deepfakes aren't just about known public figures. Researchers identified a growing ecosystem of entirely fabricated people used for political influence.

One case study: "Jessica Foster," an AI-generated blonde woman in US military uniform, appeared on Instagram in December 2025. The account posted images of Foster in barracks, in an office, and walking beside Trump in high heels. It attracted more than one million followers before being removed.

The posts linked to an OnlyFans account selling foot photos of a person who doesn't exist.

"A lot of the AI-generation is to basically get clicks and money or to drive people to a more lucrative place," said Sam Gregory, executive director of Witness, a human rights organisation. But the political messaging embedded in these profiles — pro-military, pro-Trump aesthetics — serves a dual purpose. Entertainment and ideology blur together.

## Deepfake X-rays fool doctors

The weaponisation extends beyond politics entirely. A [study published March 24](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2026/03/260326011452.htm) in Radiology found that AI-generated X-rays are realistic enough to deceive trained radiologists — even when they knew fakes were present.

The research tested 17 radiologists from 12 institutions across six countries. They reviewed 264 X-ray images, split between real scans and AI-generated ones. The doctors struggled to tell them apart.

Lead author Mickael Tordjman of Mount Sinai warned of specific threats: "A fabricated fracture could be indistinguishable from a real one." The implications range from fraudulent litigation to cybersecurity attacks where hackers inject synthetic images into hospital networks to manipulate diagnoses.

The same generative AI models that create political [deepfakes](/topics/deepfakes) can produce medical imagery that undermines the reliability of digital medical records.

## The detection gap

What connects these three fronts — warfare, elections, and medicine — is a shared vulnerability. Detection tools haven't kept pace with generation tools.

It's now "trivially easy to generate a scene that looks pretty realistic and to place real individuals into scenes," said Gregory from Witness.

The volume compounds the problem. When millions of synthetic images circulate daily, fact-checkers and platform moderators face an impossible ratio. Content reaches audiences, shapes perceptions, and embeds emotional responses long before any correction arrives.

Iran's propaganda videos get debunked. Political deepfakes get flagged. Fake X-rays get studied. But the speed of creation consistently outpaces the speed of verification.

## What this means

Deepfakes have crossed from novelty to infrastructure. They're embedded in military information operations, political campaigns, and potentially healthcare systems. The technology isn't emerging — it's arrived.

The common thread isn't any single actor or agenda. It's that synthetic media has become cheap, fast, and convincing enough to deploy at scale in any context where perception matters. And perception matters everywhere.

Understanding these mechanisms — how fabricated content reaches millions, why emotional impressions persist after corrections, how the same tools serve entertainment and propaganda simultaneously — is the starting point for navigating a world where seeing is no longer believing.
