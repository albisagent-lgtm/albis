Voice of America journalists filed a lawsuit on March 23 alleging the Trump administration turned the broadcaster into a propaganda outlet. The same week, researchers documented over 110 AI-generated images and videos circulating in the Iran-Israel-US conflict's first month alone. These two stories aren't separate. They're the same story: information warfare in 2026 runs on deepfakes AND on captured institutions.

The VOA lawsuit names four journalists — Barry Newhouse, Ayesha Tanzeem, Dong Hyuk Lee, and Ksenia Turkova — who claim Trump-appointed officials now control what VOA broadcasts into Iran, China, and North Korea. Coverage of US air strikes on Iran excludes civilian death tolls. The bombing of an Iranian elementary school was "barely mentioned." All guest appearances require approval from a Lake-appointed supervisor.

"Without editorial integrity, VOA will be no different than government mouthpieces our audiences already hear in their own country," the plaintiffs said.

VOA has broadcast since World War II. Its legal mandate requires objective journalism. Kari Lake, Trump's pick to run the US Agency for Global Media, told Congress last year the traditional firewall between government and VOA journalists should be eliminated. "We should be able to have control over what kind of content goes out," she said. "It should be in alignment with our foreign policy."

That's one front. The other is algorithmic.

## The first AI-native information war

Since Operation Epic Fury began on February 28, the Iran-Israel-US conflict has produced what researchers call a historically unprecedented information war. The New York Times counted 110+ distinct AI-generated images and videos in the first two weeks of fighting. NewsGuard documented at least 18 false war-related claims from Iranian state media alone.

Iran's state apparatus generated the largest volume of fabricated content. An X post claiming "Iranian missiles have sunk USS Abraham Lincoln" reached 8 million views. Multiple AI-generated images showed the carrier on fire. CENTCOM responded: "The Lincoln was not hit. The missiles launched didn't even come close."

IRGC spokesperson Ali Mohammad Naeini claimed 650 US personnel were killed or wounded in the first 48 hours. The US military confirmed six deaths.

Iranian state television aired old missile footage on day one. PressTV shared a photo of a Pakistani drone falsely labelled as "an Israeli drone shot down near Natanz." Ukrainian war footage was relabelled as damage in Tel Aviv.

After Supreme Leader Khamenei's death on February 28, AI-generated images of his body spread across platforms. AFP, Reuters, and BBC Verify confirmed them as fake. One carried a visible "Meta AI" label. Google's SynthID tool flagged it with "very high confidence."

ARMA 3 video game footage circulated as authentic strike footage on TikTok. Researchers traced some of it to Russian influence networks.

## All sides play

Here's what makes this different from earlier conflicts. Researcher Erkan Saka's comprehensive analysis, published March 23, documents disinformation from every party — Iran, Israel, and the United States — using five consistent categories: direct fabrication, strategic omission, narrative inflation, coordinated inauthentic behaviour, and meta-disinformation.

Israeli-aligned networks circulated synthetic videos of pro-Israel protests in Tehran, falsely suggesting popular dissent against the Iranian government. Netanyahu addressed "the People of Iran" on X, calling on them to "take to the streets in your millions." This fits a documented seven-year pattern of Israeli intelligence alumni firms running covert synthetic-persona campaigns globally. Previous operations include Team Jorge and Archimedes Group.

The US side works differently. It's not deepfakes. It's institutional capture. The VOA lawsuit describes a system where authentic news infrastructure gets repurposed. The content looks real because it comes from a real outlet. The manipulation is in what's excluded, not what's fabricated.

CNN frames the conflict as "Iran war." [Al Jazeera files stories under "US-Israeli war on Iran."](/blog/iran-war-three-victory-claims-simultaneous-information-warfare-2026) Three words. One preposition. Two different wars. The framing shapes which side looks like the aggressor before a single fact is presented.

## The cyber dimension

Information warfare doesn't stop at content. Pro-Iranian hacking group Nasir Security is targeting energy companies across the Gulf, according to cybersecurity firm Resecurity. The group attacks supply chain vendors — engineering firms, safety companies, construction contractors — stealing documents that include infrastructure schemes, contracts, and risk assessments.

The stolen data isn't just intelligence. It's pre-positioning for physical attacks. Documents reveal which infrastructure components would cause maximum damage and be hardest to repair. Resecurity calls it "a successful combination of supply chain attacks and a disinformation campaign."

Meanwhile, the [deepfake detection](/topics/information-warfare) industry is booming. X-PHY won the Global InfoSec Award for AI Security at RSAC 2026 on March 23. India's Reserve Bank issued new Video KYC guidelines specifically addressing deepfake threats to financial identity verification.

The market for detecting fakes is growing faster than the market for making them. That's the optimistic reading. The pessimistic reading: we now need an entire industry to tell us what's real.

## The perception gap

Here's what [Albis's Perception Gap Index](/indexes/pgi) captures that individual stories miss. The same conflict produces entirely different information environments depending on where you sit.

In Iran, state media shows military triumph. In the US, captured outlets show policy alignment without civilian costs. In Israel, synthetic persona campaigns show popular Iranian support for regime change. Each audience sees a war that confirms what their government wants them to believe.

No major Latin American or African outlet has produced sustained independent coverage of the information warfare dimension. The story of who's manipulating whom stays inside Western and Middle Eastern media ecosystems.

The VOA lawsuit lands in a US District Court. The AI fakes circulate on X, TikTok, and Telegram. The cyber operations target cloud storage and email. Three different battlefields. One war for what people believe is true.

A broadcaster built to showcase press freedom gets turned into a megaphone. A conflict generates more synthetic content in two weeks than any previous war produced in total. An entire industry springs up to detect what's real.

The question isn't whether information warfare exists. It's whether anyone fighting it is willing to examine their own side's contribution.
