South Korea just bought Russian naphtha for the first time since December 2022. A private Korean company completed the purchase after Washington temporarily lifted enforcement of secondary sanctions — a quiet concession that says more about the [Iran war's energy shock](/blog/iran-hormuz-two-tier-oil-access-five-friendly-nations-2026) than any official statement.

The purchase happened because the alternative was worse. South Korea's petrochemical industry — the backbone of everything from semiconductor manufacturing to hospital IV bags — is running on roughly two weeks of naphtha stockpiles. Three of the country's largest chemical plants have already shut down.

## The Rice of Industry Is Running Out

Naphtha is called "the rice of industry" in Korean. It's the petroleum derivative that becomes ethylene, which becomes plastics, textiles, rubber, food packaging, medical supplies, and semiconductor chemicals. South Korea imports 45% of its naphtha, with 77% of those imports sourced from the Middle East — through the Strait of Hormuz.

When Iran closed that strait a month ago, the supply didn't just tighten. It collapsed.

Naphtha prices surged past $1,100 per barrel, nearly doubling from pre-war levels. LG Chem shut its No. 2 naphtha cracking centre in Yeosu on March 23. Yeochun NCC shut its No. 3 plant and cut output at two others. Lotte Chemical pulled forward a maintenance shutdown by three weeks, halting 1.23 million tonnes of annual ethylene capacity at Yeosu on March 27.

These aren't obscure industrial facilities. Together, they produce the raw materials that flow into Samsung's chip fabs, Hyundai's assembly lines, and every hospital in the country.

## From Garbage Bags to IV Bags

The crisis arrived at kitchen tables before it hit boardrooms. Koreans started [hoarding garbage bags](/blog/south-korea-energy-rationing-shorter-showers-vehicle-bans-hormuz-2026) after 71% of plastics manufacturers reported receiving notices about supply cuts or suspensions. Retailers imposed two-pack purchase limits. Online sellers posted delays. Social media filled with photos of empty shelves.

Then the worry moved to hospitals. IV bags are made from polyethylene — a naphtha derivative. The Ministry of Food and Drug Safety began checking pharmaceutical company inventories and reviewing emergency regulatory measures. Syringes, medical tubing, chemotherapy delivery systems — all require the same raw material now measured in weeks, not months.

"If the conflict is prolonged, it could impact the entire hospital environment, from surgeries to chemotherapy," a pharmaceutical industry official told Seoul Economic Daily.

On March 27, the government enacted a five-month ban on all naphtha exports. Every barrel produced domestically now stays in the country, with export only possible through direct ministerial approval. Priority goes to healthcare, core industries, and essential consumer goods — in that order.

## The Sanctions U-Turn Nobody Announced

Three years ago, South Korea voluntarily stopped buying Russian crude and naphtha to stand with Western sanctions against Moscow's invasion of Ukraine. It was a principled stand from a country that didn't need to make it — South Korea isn't in NATO and faced real economic costs.

Now the principle has met the IV bag.

On March 26, Seoul confirmed the US had cleared non-dollar payments for Russian oil products. Korean companies can pay in Chinese yuan, Russian roubles, or UAE dirhams without facing secondary sanctions. The trade ministry called it a removal of "financial and sanctions barriers." Washington framed it as a temporary easing to curb global oil price spikes.

By March 30, a private Korean company had completed the first import. The government told lawmakers it's working with the foreign ministry to secure more.

The perception gap on this story is sharp: PGI 5.9 across three regions telling three different stories. In Seoul, this is survival — the world's 10th-largest economy doing what it must to keep 52 million people's hospitals and factories running. In Brussels, it's a precedent that could unravel the entire [sanctions architecture](/blog/russia-gasoline-export-ban-april-2026-global-impact) built since 2022. In Washington, it's an ally whose reliability just became a question mark.

## What Happens Next

The Russian naphtha is a stopgap, not a solution. Korean lawmakers acknowledged that scaling up Russian imports will take time — logistics, payment systems, and shipping routes all need rebuilding after three years of zero trade. Meanwhile, the Hormuz blockade continues, naphtha prices remain at record levels, and experts warn of a full energy crisis by late April.

The deeper story is structural. South Korea built the world's fifth-largest petrochemical industry on the assumption that Middle Eastern oil would always flow through a 33-kilometre strait. That assumption broke on March 1, 2026. Russian naphtha bought with Chinese yuan is the emergency patch. Nobody's talking about what comes after.

Seven days remain until Trump's April 6 Iran deadline. If the [strait stays closed](/blog/south-korea-chip-factory-triple-crisis-helium-energy-carbon), South Korea's two weeks of naphtha becomes zero weeks — and the choice between sanctions principles and hospital supplies won't be a choice at all.
