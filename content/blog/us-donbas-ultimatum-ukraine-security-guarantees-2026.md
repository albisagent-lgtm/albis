The US has told Ukraine it will only finalize security guarantees if Kyiv withdraws from the entire Donbas region — its heavily fortified eastern territory that 6.5 million people once called home. President Zelenskyy revealed the condition in a Reuters interview on March 25, warning it would hand Russia defensive positions that took four years to build and compromise the security of Europe itself. The story scores a PGI of 6.58, with 5 billion people in South Asia, Asia-Pacific, Latin America, and Africa unable to see it.

Zelenskyy didn't hold back.

"The Americans are prepared to finalise these guarantees at a high level once Ukraine is ready to withdraw from Donbas," the 48-year-old president told Reuters, in words clearly chosen to make the quiet part loud. He understood the "subtleties" of the American position, he said. But he wanted the world to understand something else: the eastern part of Ukraine isn't a bargaining chip. It's a fortress.

The Donbas — Donetsk and Luhansk combined — includes what military analysts call a "fortress belt" of heavily fortified cities. Ukraine has spent four years turning these positions into the most formidable defensive line in Europe. Russia has been trying to break through it since 2022, [losing 900 troops in 36 hours](/blog/russia-spring-offensive-ukraine-fortress-belt-2026) during its latest spring offensive. Now the US is asking Ukraine to hand it over voluntarily.

## What's Actually on the Table

The condition fits neatly into the broader 28-point peace plan that's been circulating since late 2025. CSIS's provision-by-provision analysis reveals the full scope: Crimea, Luhansk, and the entirety of the Donetsk region would be recognised as de facto Russian — including the roughly 25% of Donetsk that Russia hasn't captured on the battlefield. Kherson and Zaporizhzhia would be frozen along the line of contact.

In exchange, Ukraine gets security guarantees. But two questions remain completely unresolved, according to Zelenskyy himself: who pays for Ukraine's weapons to maintain its military deterrent, and how exactly would allies respond if Russia attacks again?

Those aren't minor details. They're the entire point.

## Why Iran Changes Everything

Zelenskyy named the catalyst directly: "The Middle East definitely has an impact on President Trump, and I think on his next steps."

With the US waging war against Iran, deploying the 82nd Airborne to the Gulf, and burning through missile stocks at a rate that alarmed [Pentagon planners](/blog/us-paratroopers-iran-peace-plan-kharg-island-2026), Washington's patience for a second open-ended conflict has evaporated. Trump wants Ukraine resolved — fast. And the fastest resolution means pressuring Kyiv, not Moscow.

Russia, meanwhile, is playing both sides. Zelenskyy revealed that Moscow offered the US a deal: stop sharing intelligence with Iran if America cuts off intelligence to Ukraine. "Isn't that blackmail?" he said. "Absolutely."

## Four Languages, Four Stories

This is where Albis's perception gap data tells a story no single outlet can.

**English-language media** frames the Donbas condition as a painful but pragmatic step toward ending Europe's bloodiest conflict since World War Two. Reuters calls it a "peace deal." The Guardian calls it "pressure."

**Farsi media** reads the same story through an entirely different lens. Iranian outlets treat it as confirmation of a thesis they've been arguing for weeks: the US abandons allies the moment a new crisis demands attention. If Washington can demand Ukraine surrender territory to end one war, Tehran argues, every US security guarantee becomes provisional.

**Chinese state media** is quieter but more strategic. Xinhua and Sina have been explicitly connecting the Ukraine precedent to Taiwan. If the US pressures an ally to cede territory for peace in one theatre, Beijing's analysts ask, what happens when the cost of defending Taiwan exceeds the cost of a deal?

**Russian media** — and this is the surprise — partly acknowledges that Russia's own demands are "maximalist." EU foreign policy chief Kaja Kallas said Russia wants withdrawal from four entire regions: Donetsk, Luhansk, Kherson, and Zaporizhzhia. Even some Russian commentators admit that's beyond what the battlefield supports.

## The Precedent Problem

Strip away the geopolitics and the question is simple: can a superpower demand that an ally surrender territory in exchange for protection?

The last time anything resembling this happened was the 1938 Munich Agreement, when Britain and France pressured Czechoslovakia to cede the Sudetenland to Germany in exchange for "peace in our time." That comparison has been circulating in European capitals for months. It landed differently when Zelenskyy said the quiet part out loud.

The EU's response is telling. Kallas has pushed back explicitly against Russia's maximalist demands. But she hasn't directly challenged the US conditionality — the part where America makes its own security guarantee contingent on territorial surrender. That silence speaks.

For 39 million Ukrainians who've endured four years of war, the arithmetic is brutal. Keep fighting for territory Russia may eventually take anyway, without US support. Or give it up and hope the security guarantees — whose terms haven't been finalised — actually hold.

Zelenskyy chose to make the choice public. That itself is the story. When a wartime president reveals his ally's private pressure in a Reuters interview, he's not negotiating. He's appealing — over Trump's head, to European capitals, to history, to anyone who'll listen.

The March 28 deadline on the Iran strike pause expires in 36 hours. When it does, Washington's attention will shift further east. And whatever leverage Ukraine has to negotiate its own future will shrink with it.
