Ninety-three days. That is how long the Arab world's cereal reserves would last if every grain ship stopped arriving tomorrow, according to a United Nations policy brief published on Wednesday and barely discussed in any major English-language newsroom.

The figure comes from ESCWA — the UN Economic and Social Commission for Western Asia — which released its second war-impact assessment on 2 April. The document lays out a scenario Arabic-language media has been reporting for weeks but most English-language audiences have not encountered: across 22 Arab nations containing more than 400 million people, food reserves cover "just over three months of consumption." The war is now five weeks old.

## A number that matters more than the oil price

Western coverage of the Hormuz crisis has been dominated by the spot price of Brent crude ($141.37, highest since 2008) and the collapse in strait transits (from 130-plus ships per day to single digits). Those numbers describe a supply problem. The three-month grain reserve figure describes what happens next.

The Arab region imports the majority of its cereals. Egypt is the world's largest wheat importer. Gulf states produce virtually none of their own grain and rely on shipments that, until five weeks ago, moved routinely through Hormuz.

ESCWA's Acting Executive Secretary Mourad Wahba warned that a 20 percent rise in global food prices — a threshold the FAO says is already within reach — would push five million more people into food insecurity across Arab middle- and low-income countries. "Without swift intervention," he said, "the compounding effects of conflict could deepen poverty, fuel social unrest in fragile countries and reverse progress towards sustainable development."

Xinhua ran the story the day it was published. Arabic-language outlets covered it extensively. In English, it landed in a handful of aggregation services and vanished.

## What Arabic media is reporting that English media is not

While English-language press has led with the first US F-15E shot down and the 40-nation coalition talks, Arabic-language economists have been tracking a different war: the one in kitchens and markets.

Regional outlets have reported food price increases of 30 to 40 percent across Arab households since the conflict began. Reuters confirmed that urea prices — the backbone of nitrogen fertiliser — have surged by the same margin. But the link between fertiliser markets and the price of bread in Cairo, Amman, or Tunis has received almost no sustained English-language reporting.

The FAO's Food Price Index rose in March to 128.5, its highest since September 2025. The biggest jumps were in vegetable oils (up 5 percent) and sugar (up 7 percent) — staples that form a disproportionate share of low-income household budgets in the Arab world.

## Egypt prepares for the worst

Egypt's Prime Minister told reporters on Wednesday that the country's reserves of "strategic goods" — wheat, vegetable oils, corn, oil, and gas — were sufficient for six months. The statement was meant to reassure. Read differently, it is an admission: the government of 105 million people is publicly counting its pantry.

Cairo has already mandated retail closures by 9pm, dimmed street lights, and slowed major construction projects. The measures echo the austerity of the 2011 crisis — a parallel no Egyptian official wants drawn, given what followed.

The UAE has said its reserves cover four to six months. But the UAE also depends on desalinated water from energy-intensive plants on the Gulf coast — the same coast where Iranian missiles struck Kuwait's Al-Zour desalination facility this week. Nearly 40 million people across GCC nations depend on desalinated water. Damage to that infrastructure would compound the food crisis with a water crisis.

## The fertiliser clock

Behind the grain reserve number sits a slower, larger threat that the International Crisis Group, the Guardian, and CNBC have all flagged but that has not yet penetrated routine English-language news cycles: the fertiliser blockade.

Roughly one-third of the world's exportable fertiliser raw materials transit Hormuz. Qatar, one of the largest urea exporters, has no alternative route. With spring planting underway across the Northern Hemisphere, every week of closure pushes the damage further into the future. Reduced fertiliser now means lower yields in late 2026 and into 2027 — a delayed fuse that won't show up in food prices for months.

Italy's foreign minister has been pushing alongside Dutch and UAE counterparts for a UN "humanitarian corridor" through Hormuz for fertiliser, modelled on the 2022 Black Sea Grain Initiative. The proposal has received modest European coverage and virtually none in the United States.

## Why you haven't seen this

The gap is structural, not conspiratorial. English-language newsrooms are covering the war through lenses of military escalation and oil markets — frames that privilege action and drama. A UN policy brief about grain reserves does not have footage or a protagonist.

But the ESCWA numbers describe a vulnerability that predates the war and that the war is now stress-testing to its limits. The Arab region's import dependence has been a known risk for decades. What has changed is that the shipping corridor is now a war zone, the energy needed to produce and transport food is at an 18-year high, and the fertiliser needed to grow next year's harvests is sitting in port.

Three months of grain. Five weeks of war. The arithmetic is not complicated. It is simply being done in a language most English-speaking newsrooms are not reading.
