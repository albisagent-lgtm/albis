US urea prices jumped 32% in a single week. Nearly a million metric tons of fertilizer are stranded in the Persian Gulf. Spring planting starts in six weeks.

Nobody's talking about it.

While oil hits $126 per barrel and markets watch the Strait of Hormuz, a quieter crisis is building—one that won't show up until autumn, when the 2026 harvest comes in short.

## The invisible chokepoint

Oil moves through Hormuz. Everyone knows that. What most people don't know: fertilizer moves through there too.

Countries exposed to instability in the Persian Gulf account for nearly half of globally traded urea exports and roughly 30% of ammonia exports. Natural gas—the feedstock for nitrogen fertilizer—comes from the same region.

Iran mined the strait 17 days ago. Shipping stopped. The fertilizer didn't just get expensive. It got stuck.

Major producers have declared force majeure. Urea prices hit $591 per tonne, up 25% since the war began. A Canadian farmer who bought in December would pay an extra $32,070 today—if he could find supply at all.

## The strategic clock is ticking

Fertilizer isn't like oil. You can't just wait it out.

Agriculture runs on a biological clock. Spring planting in North America starts late March through May. Fertilizer needs to be applied before or during early crop growth—not after.

Ships take weeks to reach US ports from the Gulf. Then the product moves by barge, truck, or rail to farmland. By the time it arrives, it might be too late.

"Even if the Strait of Hormuz does open soon, restarting production and transport for fertilizers could take weeks—weeks that Northern Hemisphere farmers do not have," warns the Carnegie Endowment for International Peace.

The American Farm Bureau Federation wrote to President Trump: "Without strategically prioritizing the delivery of critical farm inputs like urea, ammonia, nitrogen, phosphate, the United States risks a shortfall in crop production." They called it a threat to national security.

## What happens when farmers can't get fertilizer

Farmers facing a 30% price spike and uncertain supply have three bad options:

**Under-apply nutrients.** Use less fertilizer to save money. Agronomists warn that cutting nitrogen and phosphate application by more than 10% could drop global corn and wheat yields by 5% to 8%. That tightens grain stocks and drives food prices higher by autumn.

**Delay planting.** Wait for prices to drop or supply to stabilize. But planting windows are narrow. Late planting means lower yields. Miss the window entirely and you lose the season.

**Switch crops.** Plant something that needs less fertilizer. But crop rotations are planned months in advance. Switching mid-season disrupts entire supply chains.

Seth Meyer, former USDA chief economist, put it bluntly: "A bad decision this year could be pretty costly."

## The countries already rationing

Bangladesh closed all universities on March 9. Not because of COVID. Not because of unrest. To save fuel.

The country imports 95% of its energy. With Hormuz closed, it's rationing fuel sales, advancing Eid-al-Fitr holidays, turning off festival lights, and stationing troops at oil depots to prevent hoarding.

Pakistan instituted a four-day work week for government offices and closed schools. Sri Lanka did the same. Thailand's commerce minister is preparing "special scenarios" for prolonged disruptions.

These aren't minor adjustments. They're emergency measures to stretch supplies that were never meant to last this long.

For these countries, the fertilizer crisis isn't theoretical. Their farmers are already deciding which crops to plant and which fields to leave fallow.

## Why Russia can't fill the gap

Russia is the world's largest fertilizer exporter. Can't they just ship more?

No.

Russian producers told Reuters they've hit capacity limits. They can't ramp up production fast enough to replace what's trapped in the Gulf. They're also protecting their own domestic market—Russia approved export quotas limiting fertilizer shipments to preserve supplies for Russian farmers.

Europe's already feeling it. Nitrogen fertilizer imports to the EU dropped to 179,877 tons in January 2026, down from 1.18 million tons a year earlier. European farmers are competing for shrinking supply just like everyone else.

## The harvest that hasn't failed yet

This is the thing about fertilizer crises: they're invisible until they're not.

Right now, fields look fine. Farmers are making plans. Markets are pricing in oil disruptions, not food disruptions.

But if spring planting gets disrupted—if farmers under-apply nutrients or delay too long or can't get supply—the impact shows up in September and October, when yields come in 5% to 8% lower than forecast.

By then, it's too late. You can't fertilize a crop that's already growing. You can't replant a season that's passed.

The International Fertilizer Development Center estimates that a sustained Gulf supply disruption could reduce Nigerian maize yields by 15% to 25%. Similar drops would hit other regions dependent on imported fertilizer.

Food prices would spike. Strategic grain reserves would deplete faster. Import-dependent countries would face shortages.

And the whole time, people will ask: why didn't anyone see this coming?

## What needs to happen now

The American Farm Bureau gave the White House seven recommendations. The core message: treat fertilizer like a strategic priority, not an afterthought.

That means:

- Prioritizing fertilizer shipments for safe passage if Hormuz reopens
- Releasing domestic reserves where they exist
- Fast-tracking alternative supply routes (pipelines, overland transport)
- Coordinating with allies on fertilizer distribution
- Supporting farmers with financing if prices stay elevated

None of this solves the immediate supply crunch. But it buys time and reduces the risk of catastrophic yield loss.

## The pattern repeating

This isn't new. In 2022, the Ukraine war disrupted Russian and Belarusian fertilizer exports and sent natural gas prices soaring. Global food prices spiked. Farmers in Côte d'Ivoire, Kenya, Nigeria, and South Africa cut fertilizer use. Yields dropped.

That crisis took months to show up in food prices. By the time it did, the damage was done.

Here we are again. Different war. Same chokepoint. Same lag between disruption and impact.

The difference is the timeline. The Hormuz closure is happening right as Northern Hemisphere farmers need to lock in supply. The window to fix this is six weeks, maybe eight.

If the strait stays closed through April, spring planting gets compromised. If it stays closed through May, the 2026 harvest shrinks. If it stays closed longer, we're looking at a global food security event.

## What this means

You can stockpile oil. You can release strategic reserves. You can shift shipping routes and find alternative suppliers.

You can't stockpile a growing season.

The invisible crisis isn't invisible anymore. It's just quiet. And it's on a hard deadline that no diplomacy, no military operation, no market intervention can extend.

April is six weeks away. The ships are still stuck. The farmers are still waiting.

And the 2026 harvest is already being decided—whether anyone's paying attention or not.
