The United States dropped bunker-buster bombs on Iran's Natanz nuclear enrichment facility on March 21. Within hours, Iranian ballistic missiles struck the Israeli cities of Dimona and Arad — towns near Israel's own nuclear complex — wounding over 100 people. It was the first time both sides hit each other's nuclear-linked sites in a single day.

Then things got worse.

## The 48-hour clock

Late Saturday night from his Florida residence, Trump posted a threat on Truth Social: if Iran doesn't "FULLY OPEN, WITHOUT THREAT" the Strait of Hormuz within 48 hours, the US will "hit and obliterate their various POWER PLANTS, STARTING WITH THE BIGGEST ONE FIRST."

Iran's response came within hours. The Khatam al-Anbiya military command declared that if Iran's energy infrastructure is attacked, "all energy infrastructure, information technology systems, and desalination facilities belonging to the United States and the regime in the region" would be targeted.

The deadline expires around Monday, March 24.

This isn't posturing in a vacuum. Desalination plants already provide most drinking water for Gulf states. Earlier this month, plants in both Iran and Bahrain were attacked. The Atlantic Council warned the targeting of water infrastructure "forecasts a dark future" for the region. Trump's ultimatum threatens to make that future arrive this week.

## The war that ate its own sanctions

Something remarkable happened this weekend that didn't get the headlines it deserved. The US — which spent years building the sanctions architecture that squeezed Iran and Russia — quietly lifted oil sanctions on both countries within the same week.

Iranian crude on stranded tankers can now sell freely until April 19. Russian oil restrictions were eased simultaneously. The war America started forced America to dismantle the economic weapons it had spent decades building.

Meanwhile, Saudi Arabia expelled Iran's military attache and four embassy staff, giving them 24 hours to leave the kingdom. The reason: "repeated Iranian attacks" on Saudi territory. The 2023 China-brokered rapprochement between Riyadh and Tehran — once hailed as a diplomatic triumph — is now a footnote.

## Three wars on three continents

What makes this moment different from a standard military escalation is the number of systems breaking at once.

**The nuclear ladder.** Natanz and Dimona aren't just military targets. They're the physical embodiments of both nations' nuclear ambitions. Hitting them crosses a threshold that's different from bombing airfields or command centres. Israel's IDF spokesman said the campaign was at the "halfway" stage. Iran's new Supreme Leader, Mojtaba Khamenei — who was injured in the February 28 strike that killed his father — hasn't been seen in public. CIA and Mossad are actively searching for signs he's alive. There is no clear decision-maker on the Iranian side, and no off-ramp being offered.

**The kitchen-table crisis.** Three weeks into the Hormuz closure, the cascade has reached the most domestic spaces imaginable. In India, 189,000 households are on waiting lists for cooking gas. Hotels are shutting. Restaurants are trimming menus. India imports 60-65% of its LPG through the strait, and 300,000 tonnes of gas are stranded on the wrong side. The government approved Indian-flagged vessels to try alternative routes starting Monday.

In the Philippines, restaurants are closing. Sri Lanka introduced petrol rationing. Nepali migrant workers are returning home as cooking fuel dries up. Japan released 80 million barrels from strategic reserves — 45 days of supply — because 90% of its oil comes from the Middle East.

Australia has 30 days of diesel left. NSW service stations are running dry.

**The forgotten front.** While the world watches Hormuz, Russian mechanised units are 20 kilometres from Sloviansk in eastern Ukraine. Children are being forcibly evacuated. ISW reports intensified mechanised assaults ahead of a spring-summer offensive. Only European media is covering it. The US — Ukraine's main military backer — isn't reporting on the city Russia is about to encircle.

## Pakistan-Afghanistan: the ceasefire nobody trusts

On March 18, Pakistan announced a five-day pause in military operations against Afghanistan for Eid al-Fitr. Hours later, Kabul agreed to a matching suspension. Saudi Arabia, Qatar, and Turkey brokered the pause.

It holds, barely. The Kabul hospital strike that killed an estimated 400 people — Pakistan bombed what the Taliban says was an addiction treatment centre — still burns. India called it "barbaric" and sent emergency medical aid. Pakistani officials claimed the facility harboured militants, then offered the defence that "all fidayeen are drug addicts." That justification got thorough debunking in Hindi-language media and almost none in English.

The ceasefire expires March 24 — the same day as Trump's Hormuz deadline.

## What the numbers say

Brent crude closed Friday at $112.19 per barrel, up 3.3% on the day and more than 50% higher than before the war began. But benchmarks understate the reality. Abu Dhabi's Murban grade has doubled. The IEA estimates 400 million barrels have been removed from global markets — roughly four days of world supply — and warns recovery will take at least six months.

Wall Street has lost $3.2 trillion since the war began. The S&P 500 hit its 2026 low, shedding $1 trillion in the past week alone. Gold, usually a safe haven, just had its worst week in 40 years, down 18% from its all-time high.

Qatar confirmed Iranian attacks destroyed 17% of its LNG export capacity. Repairs will take five years. The country's GDP could fall 13% in 2026.

Bank of America modelled four scenarios ranging from $70 to $240 per barrel of oil. The worst case: the war drags through the second quarter.

## Who sees what

The Natanz-Dimona exchange produced at least three incompatible narratives from the same 24 hours.

In Washington: Natanz was a denuclearisation success. Dimona was unprovoked Iranian aggression.

In Arabic-language media: Natanz was an illegal attack on a sovereign nation's nuclear program. Dimona was symmetrical retaliation. Al-Araby Al-Jadeed's headline read: "Iran targets Dimona after Natanz... eye for an eye?"

In Beijing: the entire exchange was an economic catastrophe. Chinese outlets measured the war in manufacturing costs, not missile trajectories.

In New Delhi: the war barely exists as a geopolitical event. It's a cooking gas shortage. NDTV Hindi led with "10 big government steps for LPG crisis." The war is something that happened to Indian kitchens.

This is the pattern Albis tracks: the same facts, filtered through different interests, producing different realities. Right now, 52.8 million people in West Africa face food insecurity, up 21% — driven partly by war-inflated oil costs pushing up food prices. Thirty-one African countries need emergency food aid. Almost no Western outlet is reporting it.

## What to watch

The next 48 hours carry two deadlines. Trump's Hormuz ultimatum expires Monday. The Pakistan-Afghanistan Eid ceasefire expires the same day.

If neither holds, the world faces simultaneous escalation in the Gulf and South Asia — with an energy crisis that's already emptying Australian fuel stations and closing Indian restaurants providing the economic backdrop.

Six European nations and Japan signed a joint statement on Hormuz on March 19, proposing a multilateral naval coalition. But the statement contained no commitments to send ships. Trump called NATO allies "cowards" for their reluctance. Iran hit a vessel off Sharjah, UAE — 15 nautical miles from shore — on Saturday, proving it can strike beyond the strait itself.

The war's fourth week opens with two nuclear-linked sites damaged, a 48-hour countdown, a vanished Supreme Leader, and a global energy system the IEA calls the worst crisis in history. The people making the decisions can't agree on what they want. The people paying the price can't get cooking gas.
