Twenty days. Three adversaries. Three sanctions waivers.

On March 5, the US Treasury issued Russia General License 133 — authorizing Indian purchases of sanctioned Russian crude oil already on vessels at sea. On March 19, the same Treasury Department removed Belaruskali and the Belarusian Potash Company from the US sanctions list entirely, unlocking 23% of global potash reserves. On March 20, it issued a 30-day general license freeing 140 million barrels of Iranian crude oil for sale on global markets.

Each announcement came with a rationale. The Russia waiver: easing oil supply pressures. The Belarus waiver: addressing the fertilizer shortage driving food prices higher. The Iran waiver: managing the energy shock from a war still in progress.

Taken separately, each reads as crisis management. Taken together, they trace a doctrine.

## The Pattern

The three waivers share a structure. The US built each sanctions regime to achieve a strategic objective — break Russia's war economy, isolate Lukashenko after the 2020 election fraud and use of migrants as weapons, strangle Iran's nuclear and missile programs. When the Iran war drove commodity prices higher, all three regimes were unwound, sequentially, over three weeks, to manage the consequences.

The Institute for the Study of War documented the logic plainly in its March 13 assessment: the Russia waiver "will buttress the Russian war economy" and "may financially benefit Russia by reversing months of declining Russian oil revenue and allow Russia to continue to finance its war against Ukraine in the medium term."

The Belarus case is structurally identical. Belaruskali is the largest taxpayer in Belarus, providing the Lukashenko government with critical foreign currency. It holds roughly one-fifth of the world's potash supply. Removing it from sanctions increases Lukashenko's revenue at the moment the US claims to be pressuring him over election repression and Belarus's support for Russia's Ukraine operation.

The Iran case completes the trilogy. The US is currently conducting active military operations against Iran — A-10 Warthogs and Apache helicopters have destroyed over 100 Iranian naval vessels in the strait. On the same day US Marines were deploying to the Gulf, the Treasury released 140 million barrels of Iranian crude into global markets.

## Three Framings, One Decision

The pattern is legible — but only to those watching all three stories simultaneously. Most audiences aren't.

In the US, each waiver was covered as an isolated energy or food security response. Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent described the Iran waiver as a "10-14 day price buffer." The Russia waiver got similar framing: a pragmatic measure to offset the Hormuz shock. The Belarus waiver was packaged with a prisoner release — 250 political prisoners freed in exchange for sanctions relief — which dominated English-language coverage.

Ukrainian media read the same events differently. The Belarus waiver led with a single word: *betrayal*. Belaruskali's revenues fund Lukashenko. Lukashenko hosts Russian military logistics. Russian military logistics fund the Ukraine war. The chain isn't contested; it's documented. Ukrainska Pravda noted the prisoner deal took the focus — "but the financial consequences are larger than the headline."

Arabic and Russian media focused on the Iran waiver: the US was bombing Iran and buying its oil simultaneously. That framing doesn't require analytical work — it's factually accurate. The same Treasury Department that authorized the strikes facilitated their energy revenues within 24 hours.

The [Albis Perception Gap Index](/indexes/pgi) scored the Iran waiver alone at 9/10 — the highest reading of Day 21. The combined three-waiver pattern, which no single media ecosystem has assembled in full, would score higher.

## The April 19 Decision Point

The Iran waiver expires April 19. That date matters.

If the administration renews it, the pattern becomes explicit policy: the US will waive sanctions on adversaries when oil prices threaten domestic economic stability. Sanctions become contingent instruments rather than strategic ones. Every government currently under US sanctions now has a roadmap for generating the domestic price pressure that triggers relief.

If the administration lets it expire without a Hormuz resolution, Brent crude — currently around $112/barrel — climbs again. The Russia and Belarus waivers are structured similarly: the Russia GL 133 authorized oil loaded "as of March 5," with no explicit expiry language in the OFAC text beyond the 30-day delivery window. Whether it's quietly renewed or allowed to lapse will answer the same question.

The decision point is four weeks away. The answer will determine whether the waivers were crisis management or something more permanent.

## What Sanctions Now Mean

The practical question isn't whether the Trump administration intended to finance its adversaries. It's whether the outcome is meaningfully different either way.

ISW's framing is the most precise: sanctions relief "may financially benefit Russia by reversing months of declining Russian oil revenue." The conditional is diplomatic. The mechanism is clear. Oil revenues fund the Russian defense budget. The defense budget funds the Ukraine operation. The US removed the financial pressure it spent four years building, to manage the oil price effects of a war it started.

The [Russia-Ukraine sanctions history](/perspectives/russia) shows just how long and costly that pressure campaign was. Building it took international coordination across the G7, SWIFT exclusions, price caps, and secondary sanctions enforcement. Unwinding it took a general license and a Friday afternoon press release.

The Belarus and Iran waivers follow the same logic. The financial benefit flows to governments the US is simultaneously sanctioning, pressuring, or bombing. The framing in each case is food, energy, prices — never the adversary's war machine.

Sanctions have always had limits. They leak, they adapt, they produce substitutes. What's new in March 2026 isn't that sanctions are imperfect. It's that the sanctioning government is providing the relief directly.

The three waivers don't prove that US foreign policy is irrational. They prove that when sanctions conflict with oil prices, oil prices win. That's a legible doctrine. The question is whether the public it governs knows that's the doctrine — or whether the coverage of three separate press releases keeps it invisible.
