On the same week English-language feeds filled with tanker maps, missile alerts and oil-price charts, one Turkish business headline put the story somewhere else: **"The effect of war on food."**

That framing matters. It moves the Iran-Hormuz crisis from the screen to the pantry.

Turkish outlets spent early April following the conflict not through diplomacy or battlefield updates but through the price of fertilizer, the cost of transport and the likely knock-on effect on fruit, sugar and household staples. Hürriyet’s business outlet BigPara reported on 6 April that the war had pushed global food prices up 2.4 percent in March, citing Food and Agriculture Organization data, and warned that fertilizer price rises inside Turkey — ranging from 8.3 percent to 55 percent after the war began on 28 February — were expected to feed into food prices in the months ahead.

That is not how most English-language audiences encountered the same week.

In English, the dominant frame remained oil: Brent, shipping lanes, risk premiums, military escalation. Those facts are real. But Turkish coverage was doing a second-order calculation that much of the English press largely left implicit: if energy costs climb, fertilizer costs climb; if fertilizer costs climb, farm costs climb; if farm costs climb, supermarket prices follow.

The FAO’s March index supports that chain. The agency said its Food Price Index averaged 128.5 points in March, up 2.4 percent from February and the highest since September last year. Vegetable oils rose 5.1 percent month-on-month and sugar 7.2 percent. In its breakdown, FAO said commodity groups were responding not only to routine market factors but also to higher energy prices linked to the escalation in the Middle East.

Turkish reporting treated that as a domestic consumer story, not a distant macroeconomic one.

BigPara tied the war directly to Turkey’s inflation data. Transport prices, it reported, rose 4.52 percent month-on-month in March, up from 2.58 percent in February, as the Hormuz shock fed through fuel costs. Food inflation in Turkey was 1.8 percent on the month, according to the same report. The more revealing point was not the March number itself but the expectation embedded in the coverage: the bigger pass-through is still ahead, because fertilizer hits harvests with a delay.

Sabah, citing a UBS analysis, pushed the same logic further. If current energy prices hold, it reported, fertilizer prices could rise as much as 48 percent year-on-year. The bank estimated that the food-price effect alone could add around 12 percent annually to global food prices, with inflation effects reaching as high as 240 basis points in developing economies.

Reuters has separately reported that prices for nitrogen-based fertilizers such as urea have already risen 30 to 40 percent since the conflict began. That helps explain why Turkish coverage has focused so heavily on agriculture. Turkey sits close enough to the shock for energy prices to bite immediately and broad enough across food production for fertilizer costs to matter politically.

The result is a different map of the war.

In that map, the first visible damage is not at the strait itself. It is in trucking costs, wholesale markets and the input bills facing growers. The conflict becomes less a military story than a timing story: what is more dangerous, the headline price jump now or the slower harvest shock three months from now?

This framing also reveals something about information gaps. Turkish outlets are not alone in linking war, fertilizer and food. Arabic, Hindi and some Spanish-language coverage has done the same. But in English-language media the connection has often appeared only as a paragraph inside bigger market stories. It has rarely been treated as the main event.

That matters because households do not buy Brent crude. They buy bread, oil, fruit, plastic packaging and bus tickets. A war that raises fuel prices is one thing. A war that lifts the cost of moving food, growing food and wrapping food is another. Turkish coverage is describing the second problem already.

There is also a geographic reason this story is easy for English audiences to miss. The countries most exposed to imported energy and imported farm inputs often narrate inflation earlier and more concretely than the countries driving global financial coverage. By the time the story appears in English, it may arrive as a general inflation print, not as a visible supply-chain sequence with identifiable pressure points.

That is what makes this week’s Turkish reporting worth reading. It captures the middle of the chain: after the oil shock, before the consumer shock is fully priced in.

English-language readers have been told that the war is pressuring energy markets. Turkish readers have been told that it is coming for the kitchen.

So far, the Turkish framing looks closer to how ordinary people will actually experience it.
