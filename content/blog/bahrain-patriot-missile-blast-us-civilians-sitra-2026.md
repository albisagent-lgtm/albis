A US-operated Patriot missile — not an Iranian drone — likely caused the March 9 explosion that injured 32 civilians including children in Bahrain's Mahazza neighbourhood, according to a Middlebury Institute analysis commissioned by Reuters. CENTCOM had called this claim a "LIE" on the day of the blast. Two weeks later, Bahrain quietly admitted a Patriot was involved. The Albis Perception Gap Index scored this story 6.93, with the gap between [Middle Eastern and US framing](/blog/gulf-states-respond-iran-strikes-crossfire-2026) reaching 8.5 across actor-portrayal dimensions.

At 2am on March 9, a missile cut across the sky over Riffa, Bahrain's second-largest city. It angled downward. A flash. Then homes in the Mahazza neighbourhood on Sitra island shattered. Shrapnel scattered 120 metres from the blast centre. A 29-year-old Bahraini woman died. Thirty-two people were hurt, including children who needed hospital treatment.

Within hours, two official stories landed.

## The First Version

US Central Command posted on X: Russian and Iranian reports blaming a failed Patriot were a "LIE." What really happened, CENTCOM said, was an Iranian drone strike on a residential neighbourhood. Bahrain's Ministry of Interior confirmed the incident and called it "Iranian aggression."

That version held for 12 days.

## What Researchers Found

Middlebury Institute researchers Sam Lair, Michael Duitsman, and Professor Jeffrey Lewis — the same team whose [work on previous Patriot failures in Saudi Arabia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2018_Riyadh_missile_strike) challenged official claims in 2018 — analysed open-source video and satellite imagery. Their conclusion: moderate-to-high confidence that the missile came from a US Patriot battery roughly 6.4 kilometres southwest of the blast site.

The evidence trail is specific. Footage from a Riffa apartment block shows a missile travelling northeast at low altitude before detonating. Blast damage concentrated along four streets matches an aerial detonation pattern. The shrapnel spread — 120 metres — fits a Patriot warhead exploding mid-air, not a drone impact on the ground.

Wes Bryant, a former senior targeting adviser at the Pentagon, reviewed the findings: "Pretty undeniable."

Reuters showed the analysis to two additional target-analysis experts and one Patriot system researcher. None disputed the conclusions. Raytheon, the Patriot's manufacturer, didn't respond to requests for comment.

## The Admission Nobody Announced

On March 21, under questioning from Reuters, Bahrain acknowledged for the first time that a Patriot was involved. But Manama added a crucial qualifier: the Patriot had *intercepted* an Iranian drone, saving lives. Civilian casualties, Bahrain said, "were not a result of a direct impact to the ground."

There's one problem with that account. Neither Bahrain nor Washington has produced public evidence that a drone was present. No confirmed drone fragments. No imagery of a drone. The blast pattern points to a Patriot warhead — with or without a target.

This creates an uncomfortable arithmetic. A single Iranian drone costs roughly $35,000. A Patriot PAC-3 interceptor costs $4-6 million. The [same cost asymmetry](/blog/kuwait-friendly-fire-us-jets-fog-of-war-iran-2026) that's draining Gulf air defence budgets also means a $5 million missile detonated over a sleeping neighbourhood — and the weapon system meant to protect Bahraini civilians may have been what hurt them.

## Two Languages, Two Wars

The framing gap on this story is among the sharpest of the entire conflict.

RT Arabic led with: "US Patriot missile falls on residential area in Bahrain." Direct attribution. No hedging. Al Araby Al Jadeed reported researchers confirming American responsibility. Arabic coverage consistently framed the US as the source of danger to the civilians it claims to protect.

English-language coverage hedged. "Likely." "Analysis finds." "Involved in." The Patriot entered the story as an actor in an investigation, not as the cause of 32 injuries.

Al Jazeera filed the story under its "US-Israel war on Iran" tag — a framing choice that places the incident inside a broader narrative of American aggression in the region. CENTCOM's original "LIE" post, still live on X, sits alongside Bahrain's own admission that a Patriot was, in fact, involved.

Le Parisien, the French daily, was more direct than most English outlets: "The grave explosion in Bahrain ultimately caused by a missile from the American Patriot system."

## The Pattern

This isn't the first time a first version crumbled.

On February 28, a girls' school in Minab, Iran was struck during US-led operations. The death toll reached 51. Initial uncertainty gave way to a US Defense Department assessment that American forces were likely responsible, possibly due to outdated targeting data.

The sequence repeats: quick claim, slow correction, quiet admission. In Bahrain's case, the correction took 12 days and required Reuters to commission independent forensics before the official story moved at all.

## What It Means

Sitra island sits at the heart of Bahrain's energy infrastructure. The same night the Patriot detonated over homes, Iranian drones hit the nearby Bapco Energies refinery — Bahrain's only refinery, processing 380,000 barrels per day. Bapco declared force majeure within hours.

So the air defence system didn't stop the refinery strike. And the interceptor that did fire may have caused the civilian casualties that were blamed on Iran.

The name on the missile that hurt you matters. It changes who you're angry at, who you trust, who you let station weapons near your home. For 12 days, 32 injured Bahrainis — and the rest of the world — were told to be angry at Iran. The evidence now says the missile came from 6.4 kilometres away, from a battery operated by an ally.

Who gets believed first isn't always who was right.
