A mortar shell hit a house in Bajaur on Sunday, killing four brothers inside. The shell came from across the Afghan border. Their names haven't been released. In a different week, four dead civilians might've made international headlines. This week, with Dubai's airport on fire and Iran trading missiles with Saudi Arabia, a mortar in Bajaur barely registered.

That's the problem. Pakistan is fighting its [deadliest conflict with Afghanistan](/perspectives/afghanistan) since the Taliban took power in 2021, and the world isn't paying attention.

## Four Weeks, Six Provinces, No Ceasefire

The fighting started February 21 when Pakistan launched strikes against TTP and ISKP camps on Afghan soil. The Taliban hit back. Since then, cross-border fire has spread across six Afghan provinces: Khost, Paktia, Paktika, Kunar, Nangarhar, and Kandahar. Pakistani jets bombed a Kam Air fuel depot near Kandahar airport on March 13. They've struck military facilities in Kandahar overnight on March 15. They've hit the Afghan capital, Kabul.

The casualty count: at least 99 dead from both sides, according to Al Jazeera. Thirteen Pakistani soldiers, 13 Afghan soldiers, 72 Afghan civilians, one Pakistani civilian. The UN says at least 185 Afghan civilians were killed or injured between February 26 and March 5 alone. Over 115,000 Afghans have been displaced.

Every mediation attempt has failed. Qatar tried. Turkey tried. Saudi Arabia tried. China's been working backchannel diplomacy since February. Nothing's stuck. Afghanistan's Taliban accuses Pakistan of wanting to "fuel the fire of war." Pakistan says the Taliban harbors terrorists who attack its citizens. Both claim the other is targeting civilians. Both are probably right.

## The Iran War Is Eating Pakistan Alive

Here's what makes this different from every previous [Pakistan-Afghanistan](/topics/pakistan) border flare-up: Pakistan's economy is simultaneously being destroyed by a war it has nothing to do with.

Pakistan imports over 85% of its crude oil through the Strait of Hormuz. The strait's been effectively closed for 16 days. Oil is trading above $100 a barrel, up 50% since the Iran war began on March 1. Pakistan raised fuel prices 20% on March 6. Diesel went to 321 rupees per litre.

The KSE-100 stock index has fallen for seven consecutive weeks. It plunged 7% in a single session on March 9, triggering a market halt. Forward price-to-earnings ratio sits at 6.6x, a level that screams panic. Short-term inflation jumped 6.44%, driven by fuel and food.

The government ordered sweeping austerity: 50% fuel cuts for official vehicles, 60% of government cars grounded, cabinet members and lawmakers forgoing 25% of their salaries for two months. The IMF review has been delayed amid what economists are calling Pakistan's most fragile moment in decades.

## The Harvest That Might Not Come

The timing is brutal. Pakistan's spring harvest is weeks away. Agriculture accounts for 23% of GDP and 37% of the workforce. Diesel powers the tractors, the irrigation pumps, the transport trucks. At 321 rupees a litre, farmers are warning they can't afford to bring crops in.

It gets worse. Pakistan's domestic [fertilizer production](/topics/energy) has shut down. The natural gas that feeds fertilizer plants comes from Qatar, which halted LNG exports after Iranian drones struck its facilities. Carnegie Endowment analysis confirms that fertilizer firms across Pakistan, India, and Bangladesh have been forced to close. Over 1.1 million tonnes of Gulf urea exports are stuck.

If diesel costs spike food at the farm gate and fertilizer shortages hit yields, Pakistan faces a food price crisis on top of an energy crisis on top of a war. Eid al-Fitr preparations are already straining household budgets that were thin before March.

## Caught Between Two Fires

Pakistan's strategic position has never been more impossible. The Indian Express frames it bluntly: "For Pakistan, many of its foreign policy bets seem to be going wrong at the same time."

On the western border, it's fighting the Taliban. To the southwest, the Iran war threatens to spill into Balochistan, where sectarian tensions run high and the BLA insurgency simmers. Saudi Arabia, Pakistan's most important financial partner, is being hit by Iranian missiles. Field Marshal Asim Munir visited Saudi defence minister Prince Khalid bin Salman on March 7. The mutual defence pact between the two countries is under renewed scrutiny.

If Riyadh invokes that pact, Pakistan would face pressure to take sides in a war between two neighbours: Iran and Saudi Arabia. While simultaneously fighting Afghanistan. While its economy buckles under $600 million in monthly oil import costs.

Trump praised Pakistan's Prime Minister Sharif and Field Marshal Munir for fighting the Taliban. That's cold comfort when the war Trump started against Iran is the single biggest driver of Pakistan's economic collapse.

## The Invisible War

Here's a pattern worth noticing. The [Albis Global Attention Index](/indexes/gai) consistently shows Pakistan-Afghanistan coverage concentrated in South Asian and Middle Eastern media. Western outlets mention it in passing, usually buried beneath Iran and Lebanon headlines. Africa and Latin America see almost nothing.

Yet the humanitarian numbers are real: 115,000 displaced in Afghanistan, 3,000 in Pakistan, 99 dead in direct fighting, 185 civilian casualties in one week from air attacks alone. These aren't small numbers. They're just happening in the wrong place at the wrong time to get noticed.

The Pakistan-Afghanistan conflict is the world's most overlooked active war right now. It's happening in the shadow of a larger crisis that's simultaneously making it harder to fight, harder to fund, and harder to end.

## What to Watch

Three things determine where this goes. First, Pakistan's spring harvest. If diesel costs and fertilizer shortages translate into food price spikes, domestic pressure on Islamabad intensifies. Second, the Saudi defence pact. If Iran keeps hitting Saudi targets, the pressure on Pakistan to choose sides grows. Third, mediation. Every diplomatic track has failed. Without a new channel, this war grinds on while the cameras look elsewhere.

The four brothers in Bajaur didn't make the front pages. Neither did the 115,000 people who've left their homes. The question isn't whether this matters. It's whether anyone will notice before it gets worse.
