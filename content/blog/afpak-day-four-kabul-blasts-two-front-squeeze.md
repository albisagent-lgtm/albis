Before sunrise on Sunday, explosions ripped through the Kabul sky.

Taliban spokesperson Zabihullah Mujahid told residents not to worry. Afghan air defences were firing at Pakistani aircraft over the capital, he said. Stay calm.

It's hard to stay calm on day four of what Pakistan's defence minister has called "open war."

## Four Days, Two Versions

The fighting started on February 26 when Pakistan launched Operation Ghazab lil-Haq — "Righteous Fury" — hitting what it called seven TTP and ISIS-K camps near the border. Pakistan says the strikes were intelligence-based and selective. Afghanistan says they killed women and children.

Since then, the conflict has escalated at a pace neither side seems able to control.

Pakistan claims 330-plus Taliban fighters killed across 22 targets, including strikes in Kabul, Kandahar, and Paktika. The Taliban launched what they called "large-scale offensive operations" along the Durand Line. On Saturday, Afghan forces said they shot down a Pakistani fighter jet over Jalalabad and captured its pilot alive. Pakistan denies this.

Neither side's casualty figures can be independently verified. Both sides are fighting an information war alongside the real one.

## The Captured Pilot

If the Afghan account is accurate, the downed jet changes the calculation. It means the Taliban possess some anti-aircraft capability — a fact that would force Pakistan's air force to rethink how it operates along the border.

Pakistan's silence on the claim is telling. Islamabad dismissed it as "totally untrue," but offered no alternative explanation for what Afghan police and military spokespeople described in matching detail.

A captured pilot is also a diplomatic card. It can be played for leverage at the negotiating table or used to humiliate an adversary domestically. How Kabul handles this — quietly or loudly — will signal whether the Taliban want escalation or an off-ramp.

## The Mediation Scramble

Everyone wants to broker peace. Almost nobody has the leverage to deliver it.

Saudi Arabia's Prince Faisal bin Farhan spoke with Afghan Foreign Minister Muttaqi on Friday about keeping diplomatic channels open. Qatar's minister of state called both foreign ministers. Turkey's Hakan Fidan worked the phones with Islamabad, Kabul, Doha, and Riyadh. China called for a ceasefire. The UN Secretary-General demanded one.

The last ceasefire, brokered by Qatar and Turkey in October 2025, held four months. It collapsed when Pakistan struck what it called TTP hideouts on February 21. Four months of trust, gone in a single night.

One mediator is missing this round. Iran's Foreign Minister Araghchi offered to broker dialogue just days ago, invoking Ramadan as a reason for restraint. Then US and Israeli strikes hit Tehran. Iran's mediation capacity doesn't exist anymore.

## Pakistan's Two-Front Squeeze

This is the connection most headlines are missing.

Pakistan now faces active conflict on its western border with Afghanistan while its other western neighbour, Iran, is being bombed by the United States and Israel. The Iran-Pakistan gas pipeline — already a fraught project — sits in limbo. The Strait of Hormuz, through which Pakistan imports much of its energy, is under an IRGC closure broadcast. Oil prices are expected to spike when markets open Monday.

India, watching from the east, has condemned Pakistan's strikes and backed Afghanistan's sovereignty. Delhi's positioning is deliberate. Pakistan's military is stretched, and India knows it.

The Times of India called it a "double whammy." CSIS framed it more carefully: the US is "on the verge of conflict with Iran, which borders both Afghanistan and Pakistan." Reuters noted that "a wide swathe of Asia — from the Gulf to the Himalayas — is now in flux."

Pakistan's room to move is shrinking by the day. It can't sustain a prolonged air campaign while energy costs climb and diplomatic attention shifts to Iran. But backing down after declaring open war carries its own political cost.

## What the World Sees

The framing depends entirely on where you're reading.

Western outlets lead with Pakistan's "right to defend itself" — the US State Department used exactly those words. Indian media emphasises Pakistan's overreach and the sovereignty violation. Chinese coverage stresses restraint and economic risk. Al Jazeera centres the civilian cost.

Afghan media — particularly TOLONews — leads with the downed jet and Taliban resilience. Pakistani channels highlight the 330 killed fighters and the TTP threat.

Same war. Different stories. The gap between them is where misunderstanding festers and escalation finds its fuel.

## The 2,600-Kilometre Problem

The border between Afghanistan and Pakistan runs 2,600 kilometres through some of the most rugged terrain on earth. The Durand Line, drawn by the British in 1893, has never been accepted by any Afghan government — not the monarchy, not the republic, not the Taliban.

This isn't a dispute that started last week. It's a wound that reopens every generation. The TTP operates from Afghan soil because the border is, in practical terms, ungovernable. Pakistan's airstrikes can hit camps, but they can't change geography.

The question isn't whether this round of fighting will end. It will. Ceasefires come and go along the Durand Line like seasons. The question is whether anything structural changes this time — or whether both sides are simply burning through ammunition and trust until the next mediator picks up the phone.

## What Comes Next

Markets open Monday. Oil prices will tell one story. The diplomatic calendar will tell another.

If Saudi Arabia can arrange direct talks, there's a path to a pause. But Riyadh is simultaneously dealing with Iranian missile debris landing on Gulf territory. Everyone's distracted.

The fighting along the Durand Line is entering its most dangerous phase: the one where both sides have declared things they can't easily walk back, and where a single incident — a missile hitting a civilian neighbourhood, a pilot paraded on television — could lock them into a longer war.

Four days in, the guns are still firing over Kabul. The mediators are still calling. And Pakistan, squeezed between Afghanistan's mountains and Iran's collapse, is running out of room.
