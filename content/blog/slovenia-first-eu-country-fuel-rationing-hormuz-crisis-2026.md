Slovenia has become the first EU member state to impose fuel rationing since the Hormuz blockade began, capping private vehicle purchases at 50 litres per day and deploying the army to transport fuel to petrol stations. The restrictions, announced by Prime Minister Robert Golob on March 22, came after pumps ran dry across the country — driven not by a shortage of reserves, but by Austrian drivers pouring across the border to exploit Slovenia's cheaper prices.

A lorry driver at the Sentilj border crossing, arriving to find his petrol station completely empty, asked local reporters if his country was "at war."

He isn't. But the [Hormuz blockade that cut 21% of global oil transit](/blog/oil-100-barrel-hormuz-crisis-ripple-effects-2026) doesn't care about borders, and neither does panic. Slovenia's crisis didn't start with a tanker explosion or a pipeline rupture. It started with a price tag. Petrol in Slovenia costs €1.47 per litre. Across the Austrian border, the same fuel costs €1.80. Diesel sits at €1.53 versus nearly €2.00. That 25-30% gap turned a 2-million-person country into a discount fuel station for its 9-million-person neighbour.

Austrian far-right leader Herbert Kickl posted photos of Austrian-plated cars queuing at Slovenian pumps, captioning it: "Isn't this sad, that we live in a country where it has become necessary for many to go abroad so that life is cheaper?" He meant it as an attack on Austria's government. It landed as an advertisement for Slovenia's pumps.

## The Army Delivers Petrol

Golob's government moved fast. The new rules: 50 litres per day for private vehicles, 200 litres for businesses and farmers. Petrol station staff enforce the caps manually. Hungary's MOL had already imposed its own 30-litre limit days earlier.

But the strangest detail is the army. Golob authorised the Slovenian Armed Forces to deploy military tankers to move fuel from storage depots to service stations. Not because the fuel doesn't exist — "the warehouses are full," Golob said — but because the country's largest distributor couldn't get it to the pumps fast enough.

That distributor is Petrol, in which the state holds a 32.3% stake. The government accused the company of failing to resolve distribution bottlenecks and ordered an emergency inquiry into potential criminal offences by staff. Petrol rejected the accusations, blaming a "sudden surge in demand." The Interior Ministry was asked to report possible crimes to law enforcement.

A government investigating its own fuel company for sabotage, while its army delivers petrol to gas stations, while its neighbour's citizens drain the pumps. This is what an [energy crisis](/blog/philippines-energy-emergency-first-nation-iran-war-2026) looks like inside the EU.

## The Domino Question

Slovenia is small — 2.1 million people, wedged between Austria, Italy, Croatia, and Hungary. But its rationing matters because it's a precedent. No EU member state has imposed fuel rationing since the European project promised collective energy security.

The question now: who's next?

Croatia already capped fuel at €1.55 per litre. Without that cap, diesel would cost €1.86. Croatian farmers consuming hundreds of thousands of litres of blue diesel are watching prices climb toward breaking point. Hungary has price controls but faces the same underlying supply squeeze. Austria, with the highest fuel costs in the region, is haemorrhaging demand across its southern border.

Russian media connected Slovenia's rationing to Bangladesh, Sri Lanka, and Myanmar — countries that imposed similar measures in the same week. Turkish outlets framed it as the "first domino" of a global fuel crisis. The pattern they're pointing at is real: the [Philippines declared a national energy emergency](/blog/chile-fuel-prices-54-percent-hike-mepco-buffer-breaks-2026) days earlier, South Korea told citizens to take shorter showers, and Chile's fuel buffer collapsed. Rich and poor countries are hitting the same wall simultaneously.

## Election Day at Empty Pumps

The timing could hardly be worse — or more revealing. Slovenians voted in a national election on the same Saturday the rationing began. Prime Minister Golob, a liberal, faced Janez Janša, a right-wing populist allied with Hungary's Viktor Orbán.

Golob won, barely. His Freedom Movement took 28.6% and 29 seats. Janša's SDS took 28% and 28 seats. In 2022, Golob had won 41 seats in a landslide. The fuel crisis didn't cost him the election, but it cut his majority nearly in half.

Coalition talks will now determine whether Slovenia gets a liberal or populist government — with fuel rationing as the backdrop. If Janša assembles a coalition, Orbán gains another ally in the European Council, joining Slovakia and the Czech Republic in a pro-Moscow bloc. The fuel queue at Sentilj just became a geopolitical fault line.

## What Three Media Worlds See

The perception gap on this story runs at a PGI of 5.75 — three distinct framings depending on where you read.

EU media treats it as crisis management. The BBC's Balkans correspondent filed a measured piece from Ljubljana, noting the "fuel tourism" phenomenon and Golob's reassurances. European coverage emphasises that reserves exist and the issue is distribution, not supply.

Russian media tells a different story. RBC Ukraine and Pravda connected Slovenia's rationing to shortages across the developing world, arguing that "pro-Western" Croatia and Slovenia are struggling while Budapest and Belgrade maintain stability through "responsible policies." The implicit message: alignment with the West means empty pumps.

Middle Eastern and Turkish media frame it as vindication. Turkey's Star newspaper called it the first proof that the Hormuz blockade can break even wealthy European economies. If Iran's selective reopening of the strait doesn't hold, the EU's solidarity mechanisms face a test they weren't designed for.

Five billion people in South Asia, Asia-Pacific, Latin America, and Africa saw none of these framings. For them, this story doesn't exist.

## What Comes Next

The Balkans have enough reserves for one to two months at current consumption. If the Hormuz blockade tightens again — and the March 28 ceasefire deadline is three days away — the "card system" modelled on Slovenia's rationing could spread across Central Europe.

The EU was built so no member state would face scarcity alone. Slovenia just proved that promise has a price cap, a daily limit, and an army tanker parked outside a petrol station in Sentilj.
