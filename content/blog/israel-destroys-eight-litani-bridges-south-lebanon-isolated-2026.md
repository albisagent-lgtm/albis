Eight bridges over Lebanon's Litani River have been destroyed or damaged in three weeks. South of the river, tens of thousands of people are running out of ways to get food, fuel, and medicine.

Israeli Defence Minister Israel Katz ordered the military to destroy all crossings over the Litani and accelerate the demolition of homes near the border. He said the strategy followed "the model we applied in Beit Hanoun and Rafah" — the [Gaza buffer zones](/topics/middle-east) where entire neighbourhoods were flattened.

Lebanon's President Joseph Aoun called it a "prelude to ground invasion."

The bridges aren't military installations. They're how families drive to hospitals, how trucks deliver cooking gas, how teachers get to schools. The Qasmiyeh Bridge, struck on Sunday, was the main link between Lebanon's south and the coastal city of Tyre. The Dallafeh Bridge, hit on Monday, connected communities 30km from the Israeli border. The Zrariyeh, Qantara, Khardali, Qaaqaait al-Jisr — each name is another road closed, another town cut off.

In Tyre, a resident named Sami Baradhi told The National: "I'm ready for all scenarios. I will eat fish from the sea. I have a solar-powered home and I've stockpiled some supplies. I will not leave."

In Qlayaa, the mayor said residents had stocked food and diesel. "Whatever happens, we are staying," he said. "We will not leave our land."

Human Rights Watch researcher Ramzi Kaiss put it plainly: if every bridge is struck, south Lebanon becomes "isolated from the rest of the country" and people "won't be able to access food, medicine and other basic needs." He called wholesale home demolitions wanton destruction — a war crime.

Here's where the [perception gap](/indexes/pgi) gets sharp. Arabic-language outlets — Al Arabiya, Al Jazeera, Asharq Al-Awsat — name every destroyed bridge individually and call this siege warfare. They use the word "islands" (جزر): south Lebanon is being turned into disconnected islands, each surrounded by rubble. Lebanese media quotes Aoun calling it collective punishment.

English-language coverage mostly frames these as "strikes on infrastructure used by Hezbollah." The BBC reported two bridges destroyed; by the time The National counted, the number was eight. The gap between "bridge strikes" and "siege warfare" is the gap between a tactical operation and a humanitarian catastrophe.

Finance Minister Bezalel Smotrich said the Litani should become the new border. "Just as we control 55 per cent of Gaza, we must do the same in Lebanon," he said. A member of his party, Zvi Sukkot, went further: "We must conquer territory in southern Lebanon, destroy the villages there and annex the territory."

Lebanon's health ministry counts 1,029 dead, including 120 children and 40 medical workers, and more than one million displaced in three weeks.

The Israeli army chief said the operation against Hezbollah "has only begun" and would be "prolonged."

South of the Litani, people aren't waiting for permission to survive. They're stockpiling rice, filling jerrycans, rigging solar panels. Bridge by bridge, the world outside is getting further away.
