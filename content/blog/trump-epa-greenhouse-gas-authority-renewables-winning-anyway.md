The Environmental Protection Agency can no longer regulate greenhouse gases. Meanwhile, the United States is installing more clean energy than at any point in its history. Welcome to the paradox of 2026.

On February 12, the EPA formally revoked the "endangerment finding"—the 2009 scientific determination that greenhouse gases threaten human health and welfare. With it went the legal foundation for federal climate regulation. The agency that once set emissions limits for power plants can no longer touch carbon pollution from major sources.

One month later, the U.S. Energy Information Administration announced that the country will add **86 gigawatts** of new generating capacity in 2026—the largest single-year increase in over two decades. Solar accounts for 51%. Batteries for 28%. Wind for 14%. Fossil fuels? Almost none.

That's a 62% jump from 2025. Renewables will provide virtually all new electricity generation this year. And it's happening while the federal government actively dismantles every climate regulation it can find.

## How we got here: 19 years in 19 seconds

In 2007, the Supreme Court ruled in *Massachusetts v. EPA* that greenhouse gases are air pollutants under the Clean Air Act. The EPA must regulate them if they endanger public health.

In 2009, the EPA issued the endangerment finding: yes, carbon dioxide and other greenhouse gases pose a danger. Federal regulation followed—limits on power plant emissions, vehicle standards, industrial sources.

In 2022, the Supreme Court's *West Virginia v. EPA* decision began chipping away at that authority, ruling the agency couldn't force power plants to shift from coal to cleaner sources. It was a warning shot.

In 2026, the Trump administration finished the job. EPA Administrator Lee Zeldin called the endangerment finding a regulation that "targeted the American dream." The endangerment finding, he said, is "hereby eliminated."

## What it means: No federal floor

The EPA can no longer:
- Set carbon limits for power plants
- Regulate greenhouse gas emissions from fossil fuel facilities
- Require industries to reduce climate pollution

States can still act. California, New York, Vermont, and others have their own climate laws and enforcement powers. But there is no longer a **federal floor**—a baseline level of protection that applies nationwide.

Trump called it "the single largest deregulatory action in U.S. history." Climate advocates called it a catastrophe. Both are right.

## The paradox: Economics already won

Here's what nobody expected: **it doesn't seem to matter**.

The U.S. is adding 86GW of clean energy in 2026. That's nearly double 2025's 53GW. Solar and wind installations are cheaper than building new fossil fuel plants in most markets. Battery storage costs have plummeted. Private investment is pouring in—not because of government mandates, but because the **economics work**.

According to the International Energy Agency, 81% of new renewable power capacity globally in 2023 was more cost-effective than fossil fuel alternatives. That trend accelerated in 2024 and 2025. By 2026, renewables aren't just competitive—they're **dominant**.

Developers are building solar and wind because they're **faster to construct**, **cheaper to operate**, and **what the market wants**. The Iran war's oil shock made energy independence a national security priority overnight. Renewables became the answer—not through climate policy, but through **strategic necessity**.

Canary Media summed it up: "Even as the Trump administration has employed novel and at times legally dubious means to block renewable energy growth, the power sector keeps choosing clean energy again and again—attracted by its low costs, speed to build, and climate and environmental benefits."

## If economics won, does policy matter?

Yes. Here's why:

**1. Speed matters.** Every year without strong federal climate action means more warming, more extreme weather, more lives disrupted. Economics drives adoption, but policy can **accelerate** it. The Inflation Reduction Act's tax credits sped deployment by years. Gutting regulations slows nothing, but also **builds nothing faster**.

**2. Fossil fuels aren't dead yet.** While renewables dominate **new** capacity, the U.S. still relies heavily on existing coal and gas plants. Without EPA authority, those plants face no federal pressure to retire or reduce emissions. They can keep polluting indefinitely.

**3. Regional inequality deepens.** Blue states with strong climate laws will charge ahead. Red states without them may lag, locked into aging fossil infrastructure while competitors build cheaper, cleaner grids. Federal policy would have **equalized** that transition.

**4. Global leadership collapsed.** The U.S. once drove international climate negotiations. Now it's sidelined itself. China, Europe, and others are building the clean energy economy—and writing the rules—without American input.

**5. The Supreme Court hasn't ruled yet.** This fight goes back to the courts. States are already suing, arguing the endangerment finding can't be revoked because the underlying science hasn't changed. If the Supreme Court upholds the Trump EPA's move, federal climate authority could be dead for a generation.

## What's next: Two futures

**Future 1: Economics finishes the job.**  
Renewable costs keep falling. Market forces drive fossil fuels into irrelevance. The U.S. decarbonizes without federal policy—slowly, unevenly, but inevitably. Climate damage mounts in the meantime, but the transition happens.

**Future 2: Economics stalls.**  
Grid constraints, permitting delays, and fossil fuel lobbying slow deployment. Without federal mandates, coal and gas plants stay online. Emissions plateau instead of falling. The world warms past 2°C. The transition happens too late.

We're living in the gap between those futures right now.

## The bottom line

Trump stripped the EPA's power to fight climate change. The U.S. is building more clean energy than ever. Both are true. Both matter.

If the Supreme Court kills federal climate authority for good, we'll find out whether economics alone can save us. The experiment is underway. The results will take decades.

One thing is certain: the market chose clean energy before the government did. The question is whether that's enough.
