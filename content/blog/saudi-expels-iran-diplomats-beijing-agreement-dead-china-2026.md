Three years ago, China pulled off something the United States had failed to do for decades. In a Beijing conference room in March 2023, Chinese diplomats brokered a handshake between Saudi Arabia and Iran that reopened embassies, reactivated a security pact, and prompted headlines about a "new era" in Middle East diplomacy. Wang Yi, China's top diplomat, called it "a victory for dialogue and peace."

On Saturday, Saudi Arabia's foreign ministry killed that victory with five words buried in a statement: "violation of the Beijing Agreement."

## The Expulsion

Riyadh declared five Iranian diplomats — including the military attaché — persona non grata. They have 24 hours to leave the kingdom. The formal statement, carried by the Saudi Press Agency, didn't mince words: Iran's "continued targeting" of Saudi sovereignty, civilian sites, economic interests, and diplomatic premises constitutes "a flagrant violation of all relevant international conventions."

But it's what came next in the text that matters most. The Saudis specifically cited violations of three frameworks: international law, UN Security Council Resolution 2817 (adopted March 11, 2026, condemning Iran's attacks on seven Arab states), and the Beijing Agreement.

That last citation is the kill shot — and most English-language coverage missed it entirely.

## What the Beijing Agreement Was

In March 2023, Saudi and Iranian security officials met secretly in Beijing for four days of talks. China positioned itself as the honest broker the region needed. The deal they struck restored full diplomatic relations, reopened embassies in Riyadh and Tehran, and reactivated a dormant 2001 security cooperation agreement. More than 85 UN member states welcomed it.

For Beijing, the deal was proof of concept. China could do what America couldn't — bring stability to the Middle East through dialogue rather than military force. Western analysts debated whether a "new era of geopolitics" had begun. Georgetown's Journal of International Affairs called it "a test case of China's role as an international mediator."

The test failed.

## How It Fell Apart

The Beijing Agreement collapsed when Iran struck Saudi territory on March 1, 2026, in the opening salvos of its retaliation against the US-Israeli bombing campaign. Since then, Iranian missiles and drones have hit ports, oil refineries, energy infrastructure, and even [the CIA station in Riyadh](/blog/us-defense-industry-hormuz-sulfur-supply-chain-crisis-unseen-gai-2026). Greek-operated Patriot systems intercepted two Iranian ballistic missiles targeting Saudi oil refineries. A drone fell at the SAMREF refinery in the Eastern Region. Explosions rocked the capital as air defenses shot down waves of incoming projectiles.

Iran apologised to its Arab neighbours. It made no difference. As Foreign Policy reported, "the situation remains fragile and unresolved, as a strongly worded statement of condemnation from Saudi Arabia made clear."

Saturday's expulsion removed any remaining ambiguity. The Beijing Agreement isn't just strained — it's dead.

## China's Uncomfortable Silence

Here's where the story gets interesting. Beijing brokered this deal. Beijing claimed credit for it. Wang Yi said it would drive "a wave of reconciliation" across the Middle East.

But when Iranian missiles started hitting the very countries China had promised to help reconcile, Beijing went quiet. Chinese officials responded with carefully calibrated statements saying Gulf nations' territorial integrity "should be respected" — without specifying by whom. They praised the GCC's "desire for peace" while calling for "all sides to deconflict."

As Foreign Policy's analysis put it: China "has been forced to strike a much meeker tone" when it comes to Iranian attacks on Arab neighbours. The country that doesn't do alliances as a matter of principle found itself caught between its partner (Iran) and its customers (the Gulf states, who buy over $107 billion in Chinese goods annually).

The Albis Perception Gap Index scored Saudi Arabia's diplomat expulsion at 6.6, with the widest gap between Middle Eastern and US coverage — Arabic media framed the story within the collapse of the Beijing Agreement framework, while English-language outlets treated it as a routine diplomatic response to attacks.

## What This Means

Three things are now clear.

**China's Middle East mediation model is broken.** The Beijing Agreement was supposed to prove that economic interdependence could keep the peace. It lasted less than three years. The next time Beijing offers to mediate a regional dispute, every party at the table will remember that China couldn't — or wouldn't — enforce the deal when it mattered.

**The Gulf states are picking sides.** Saudi Arabia's expulsion, combined with the UAE intercepting Iranian missiles and drones, Qatar coming under drone attack, and Bahrain sponsoring UNSCR 2817, shows the GCC lining up firmly against Iran. The careful neutrality that characterised the Beijing Agreement era is over.

**The three-way story is invisible in English.** If you read only English-language coverage, you saw a diplomatic expulsion — one country punishing another for attacks. Arabic media told a different story: the death of a Chinese-brokered peace framework and the failure of an alternative to American power. That's a much bigger story, and it's hiding in plain sight.

## What's Next

Trump's 48-hour ultimatum for Iran to reopen the Strait of Hormuz expires Monday. The IRGC has counter-threatened to close the strait completely if US strikes hit power plants. Saudi Arabia, now without Iranian diplomats on its soil for the first time since 2023, has no direct channel to Tehran.

The one country that could still serve as a back channel — China — has spent the past three weeks proving it won't choose between its partner and its customers. That was the Beijing Agreement's promise: that nobody would have to choose. The war made the choice for them.
