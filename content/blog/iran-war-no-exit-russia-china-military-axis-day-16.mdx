Iran's Foreign Minister Abbas Araghchi sat on CBS's *Face the Nation* on March 15 and said what intelligence agencies have been claiming for weeks: Russia and China are providing "military cooperation" to Tehran. "We had good cooperation with these countries — politically, economically, even militarily," he said. It was the first on-the-record confirmation from a senior Iranian official. The three-power axis against the United States is no longer a theory. It's policy.

On the same broadcast, Araghchi killed any hope of a diplomatic off-ramp. "We never asked for a ceasefire," he said. "And we have never asked even for negotiation. We are ready to defend ourselves as long as it takes."

Trump, for his part, told NBC that terms "aren't good enough yet."

Day 16 of the Iran war ended with both sides refusing to talk, no mediator, no channel, and no framework for stopping. Oman's foreign minister confirmed the pre-war nuclear negotiations that were "making progress" collapsed the moment bombs fell. The last diplomatic bridge is rubble.

## The Moscow Mystery

While Araghchi was projecting defiance on American television, a stranger story was unfolding behind the scenes. Kuwaiti newspaper *Al-Jarida* reported — and the *Daily Mail*, *Mirror*, and Indian intelligence sources subsequently corroborated — that Mojtaba Khamenei, Iran's new Supreme Leader, was secretly flown to Moscow on a Russian military aircraft for emergency leg surgery.

The report claims he was wounded in the opening strikes on February 28 that killed his father, Ayatollah Ali Khamenei. He's reportedly recovering in a private medical facility inside one of Putin's residences.

Iran's foreign ministry says there's "no problem." Trump previously said Mojtaba was "not alive." Defence Secretary Hegseth described him as "wounded, likely disfigured."

If the reports are accurate, the implications are enormous. The IRGC — Iran's military-political-economic apparatus — is running the country with no supreme clerical authority. That hasn't happened since 1979. And Russia, by housing the Supreme Leader, holds extraordinary leverage over Iran's war decisions.

## What Russia and China Are Actually Providing

Araghchi's admission confirmed what the [Institute for the Study of War](https://understandingwar.org/research/middle-east/iran-update-special-report-march-14-2026/) and CNN had separately reported:

**Russia**: intelligence on US military positions, advanced drone tactics for attacks on American forces, and diplomatic cover. Moscow abstained on (rather than vetoed) the UNSC resolution condemning Iran's Gulf strikes, but offered an alternative resolution calling for a ceasefire without naming parties. It was rejected.

**China**: missile spare parts, satellite intelligence via the Kanopus-V/"Khayyam" satellite system, and BeiDou navigation data. Beijing also abstained at the UN, evacuated 3,000 citizens from Iran, and has maintained what it calls "strategic caution."

Russia told Trump's envoy Keith Kellogg that it has *not* shared intelligence with Iran. Araghchi's own words contradict this directly.

The framing of this development splits cleanly by region. Western outlets lead with "axis of evil" language and the threat to US interests. [Middle Eastern coverage](/perspectives/iran) frames it as legitimate alliance-building under attack. Chinese state media — Xinhua, CGTN — treats it as factual cooperation without editorial heat. Ukrainian media, unsurprisingly, highlights the connection between Russian support for Iran and Russian aggression in Europe.

## The War's Physical Limits

Beyond diplomacy, the war is hitting material constraints on both sides.

The IDF claims 80% of Iran's air defences and 70% of its ballistic missile launchers have been destroyed. CENTCOM has struck 5,500+ targets and sunk 90+ Iranian vessels. Iran's retaliatory capacity is degraded but not eliminated — it launched two missile salvos at Israel overnight on March 15, and an [Iranian drone hit a fuel tank near Dubai International Airport](/topics/iran) on March 16, shutting down the world's busiest international airport.

On the American side, the Financial Times reported the US has "drained years of critical ammunition." Kevin Hassett, the NEC Director, put the war's cost at $12 billion in 16 days. The Pentagon is preparing a $50 billion supplemental request. Congress has been largely sidelined — the House voted 219-212 against halting the war, and a Senate war powers resolution was defeated.

Trump called on the UK, China, France, Japan, and South Korea to send warships to secure the Strait of Hormuz. The response, according to multiple outlets, has been "muted." Britain is considering sending minehunting drones. No warships.

## The Two-Tier Strait

Hormuz itself is hardening into a two-tier system. Chinese and Indian tankers pass through. Western-allied shipping doesn't. An Iranian supertanker pushed through for China last week. Two Indian LPG carriers crossed Saturday.

This isn't just a blockade. It's a real-time demonstration of what post-American energy architecture looks like. Every day the arrangement persists, it becomes harder to reverse.

Brent crude spiked to $106.50 on Monday's Asian open — up more than 50% since the war began. WTI briefly touched $100.

## Pakistan's Squeeze Tightens

The war's economic shockwaves are compounding [Pakistan's existing crisis](/perspectives/pakistan). Already fighting a grinding border war with Afghanistan across six provinces, Islamabad raised fuel prices 20% on March 6 to prevent hoarding. Diesel costs are now hitting the spring harvest — agriculture accounts for 23% of Pakistan's GDP and 37% of its workforce.

The KSE-100 stock index fell for a seventh consecutive week. The forward price-to-earnings ratio sits at 6.6x — the market equivalent of a distress signal. The IMF review has been delayed. The government imposed austerity: 50% fuel cuts for official vehicles, 60% of government cars grounded, cabinet members taking 25% salary cuts.

Pakistan faces a particular structural trap. Its Saudi defence pact could be activated if Iran's Gulf strikes continue hitting Saudi territory. That would force Islamabad to choose sides in the Iran war while simultaneously fighting Afghanistan and managing economic collapse. The Indian Express put it plainly: "For Pakistan, many of its foreign policy bets seem to be going wrong at the same time."

Gulf fertiliser exports have halted — over 1.1 million tons of urea stuck in ports. If the blockade continues through planting season, Pakistan's agricultural output faces direct damage on top of the diesel price shock. The Iran war's oil disruption feeds Pakistan's fuel crisis, which feeds the agricultural crisis, which feeds the food price crisis. It's the same system.

## No Exit

The war's trajectory is now shaped by three clocks ticking at different speeds.

**Iran's military clock** is running down. With 80% of defences and 70% of launchers reportedly destroyed, Tehran's capacity to fight degrades daily. But political will hasn't broken. Araghchi's defiance on CBS wasn't performative — it signals that the IRGC, now operating without clerical oversight, will keep fighting regardless of losses.

**America's political clock** is ticking faster than expected. $12 billion spent, ammunition stocks depleted, no allies sending warships, Congress "an afterthought" per the LA Times. The $50 billion supplemental request will be a flashpoint. Over 251 organisations have already come out against war funding.

**The regional clock** keeps accelerating. Dubai's airport shut down. Ras Tanura hit for a second time. Bahrain's sirens going off. Gulf states are calculating the cost of hosting US forces, and the maths is changing daily.

Araghchi's triple message on Saturday was designed for all three clocks simultaneously: we won't stop, our allies are with us, and we can outlast you. Whether that's true is a separate question. But with zero diplomatic channels and both sides publicly committed to not talking, the answer won't come from negotiation.

It'll come from exhaustion.
