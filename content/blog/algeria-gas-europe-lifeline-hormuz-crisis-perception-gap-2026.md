French gas imports from Algeria jumped from 65,000 tonnes to 108,000 tonnes in the first weeks of March 2026.

That's a 66% increase in weeks, not months. It happened because five Qatari LNG cargoes bound for Europe were put at risk when QatarEnergy declared force majeure after the Ras Laffan strikes. The Strait of Hormuz — where 20% of the world's LNG passes — is effectively closed. And Algeria's gas doesn't touch Hormuz.

It flows through pipelines under the Mediterranean.

## The Pipeline Advantage Nobody Talks About

The global energy crisis has a geography problem. Most of the analysis focuses on oil barrels, LNG spot prices, and the [IEA's 400-million-barrel reserve release](/blog/hormuz-strait-food-crisis-100-million-people-oil-framing-2026). The conversation is dominated by seaborne trade: tankers, chokepoints, insurance premiums that have spiked over 1,000%.

But there's another energy route that doesn't use ships at all.

Algeria sends gas to Italy through the 32.7 billion cubic metre per year TransMed pipeline, running through Tunisia. It sends gas to Spain through the Medgaz pipeline. Its LNG terminals at Arzew and Skikda load cargoes that cross the Mediterranean in hours, not the weeks required to route around Africa from the Gulf.

None of this infrastructure touches the Strait of Hormuz. None of it faces maritime insurance spikes. None of it can be blockaded by the IRGC.

Italy's Energy Minister Gilberto Pichetto Fratin told media Rome is assessing replacement gas volumes from Libya and Algeria. Spain gets 29-34% of its gas from Algeria — already its largest pipeline supplier. And France's import surge suggests the shift is already happening on the ground, faster than policy announcements.

## Algerian Media Sees a Triumph. European Media Sees a Spreadsheet.

Here's where the [perception gap](/indexes/pgi) opens.

Read Francophone Algerian media — ObservAlgérie, L'Algérie Aujourd'hui, BeurFM — and the framing is triumphant. "Algeria comes to Europe's rescue." The coverage highlights specific tonnage data, names the ports (Arzew, Skikda), and celebrates the strategic vindication of Mediterranean pipeline infrastructure that Western energy analysts spent years dismissing as insufficient.

Read European English-language media, and Algeria appears as a footnote. Reuters mentions "pipeline from Norway, Algeria and to a much smaller degree from Russia." Algeria is listed third, after Norway, as one of several supply adjustments. No tonnage. No celebration. No sense that this is a turning point.

Same shift. Two completely different stories.

The Algerian version: a former colony is rescuing its former coloniser's energy supply, and the Mediterranean infrastructure that Algeria built and maintained is proving its worth while the world's most expensive LNG routes collapse.

The European version: we're diversifying supply sources. Algeria is one of several options.

## The Numbers Behind the Shift

Algeria is Africa's largest gas producer. The sector makes up 90% of the country's exports, 60% of its budget, and 30% of GDP. In normal times, Algeria produces about 100 billion cubic metres of gas per year.

The problem: half goes to domestic consumption. The TransMed pipeline to Italy runs at or near capacity. Medgaz can deliver 10 billion cubic metres per year to Spain. Algeria's Arzew and Skikda LNG terminals have nameplate capacity of 25-29 million tonnes per year, but effective operational capacity is closer to 14-18 million tonnes due to ageing equipment.

This means Algeria can increase volumes at the margins — the French surge proves that — but can't replace Qatar's 77 million tonnes per year of LNG capacity overnight. The $60 billion investment plan announced for 2025-2029, involving Chevron, ExxonMobil, and Sinopec, won't deliver major production growth for years.

The bigger play is the Trans-Saharan Gas Pipeline, a long-planned project connecting Nigerian gas fields through Niger to Algeria and then to Europe. Algeria announced construction would begin in 2026. If completed, it would deliver 30 billion cubic metres per year — a pipeline connecting West Africa to Europe that bypasses every maritime chokepoint in the world.

## What 3.98 Billion People Don't Know

The US, South Asia, Asia-Pacific, and Latin America — 3.98 billion people — have virtually no coverage of Algeria's gas surge. The story exists in European policy circles and Algerian domestic media. That's it.

This matters because the Hormuz crisis isn't just about oil prices. It's about which countries emerge from this war with more geopolitical leverage and which emerge with less.

Algeria is gaining leverage. Its fixed infrastructure means Europe needs it more every day the Hormuz closure continues. Every week of conflict strengthens Algeria's hand in future gas contracts, pricing negotiations, and the broader EU-North Africa relationship.

This is a structural realignment, not a temporary supply swap. And the populations most affected by energy prices — in South Asia, Southeast Asia, Latin America — can't see it happening.

## The Mediterranean Advantage

The deeper lesson is about infrastructure design.

LNG was supposed to be the future of energy trade. Flexible, ship-anywhere, market-responsive. The Hormuz crisis exposed the flaw: LNG is only as secure as the sea lanes that carry it. When [Iran turned Hormuz into a toll booth](/blog/iran-hormuz-transit-fee-shipping-system-international-law-2026), every LNG cargo in the Gulf became a hostage.

Pipelines can't be rerouted, but they also can't be blockaded. They don't need insurance premiums. They don't need warship escorts. The TransMed pipeline to Italy has operated since 1983. Forty-three years of gas delivery, through wars, revolutions, and political crises across North Africa — and it's still pumping.

Algeria's moment isn't an accident. It's the payoff from decades of infrastructure that was built for exactly this kind of crisis, even if nobody planned for it.

The question isn't whether Algeria can replace the Gulf. It can't. The question is whether this crisis permanently redraws Europe's energy map — elevating Mediterranean pipeline routes from backup to backbone.

Algerian media already has an answer. European media hasn't asked the question yet.

That gap — between celebration and footnote — is the space where the next decade of Euro-African energy politics will be decided. And right now, most of the world can't see it.
