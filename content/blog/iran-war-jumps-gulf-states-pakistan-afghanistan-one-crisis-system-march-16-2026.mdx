A drone hit near Dubai International Airport on Monday night, forcing the world's busiest international hub to suspend flights. Saudi Arabia intercepted 31 Iranian drones and missiles overnight. Air raid sirens sounded across Bahrain. Hundreds of miles east, Pakistan's air force struck Taliban military facilities in Kandahar for the second time in three days.

These aren't separate stories. They're one system breaking apart.

## The war nobody can contain

Two weeks into the US-Israeli war on Iran, the conflict has done what military planners feared most: it jumped its borders. Iran's strikes on Gulf states represent a direct attack on countries that had explicitly told Tehran none of their bases would be used against it. At least 18 people have been killed across the Gulf so far.

The Guardian reported that Gulf states are furious. They didn't start this war. They tried to stay out of it. Iran struck them anyway, targeting airports, oil refineries, ports and hotels. Saudi Crown Prince Salman called the attacks "cowardly." Iran's IRGC called them self-defense.

Neither side is wrong about the facts. They're describing the same events through completely different lenses. And there's nobody mediating between them.

Iran's foreign minister, Abbas Araghchi, told CBS on Sunday: "We have never asked for a ceasefire, and we have never asked even for negotiation." Hours later, Trump told NBC that Iran wants to negotiate but "the terms aren't good enough yet." Oman and Egypt tried to broker talks. Both sides said no.

## Pakistan's two-front squeeze

While the world watches the Gulf burn, Pakistan is fighting a war of its own. Its air force struck Taliban military facilities in Kandahar overnight Saturday, hitting what security sources described as "technical support infrastructure and an equipment storage facility." The Taliban said the strikes damaged a drug rehabilitation centre and an empty container.

Pakistan's president said Afghanistan "crossed a red line" after Taliban drones hit three locations inside Pakistan on Friday night. The two neighbours have been exchanging strikes since late February.

Here's what connects the two conflicts: oil.

Pakistan imports over 85% of its crude through the Strait of Hormuz. The strait is effectively closed. Fuel prices have been raised 20% to curb hoarding. Farmers say they can't afford diesel ahead of the spring harvest. The New York Times reported that Pakistani agriculture — the sector that employs 40% of the workforce — is heading into planting season without affordable fuel.

Pakistan isn't just fighting Afghanistan. It's fighting Afghanistan while its economy crumbles under the weight of someone else's war.

## The food crisis nobody's pricing in

The Gulf fertilizer freeze may be the most underreported consequence of this war. Over 1.1 million tons of urea exports are stuck in Gulf ports. Fertilizer prices jumped 6.5% in a week. Palm oil is up 9%.

This doesn't hit immediately. It hits in two seasons, when crops planted without adequate fertilizer produce smaller yields. South Asia and sub-Saharan Africa — regions that import the most Gulf-produced fertilizer — will feel it first.

Bloomberg described the scene across Asia: "From metal furnaces in western India to rice paddies in Southeast Asia, billions of people are dealing with gas outages, drawing up crisis menus and preparing for fuel rationing." ASEAN has demanded a ceasefire. Nobody's listening.

## The information fog

Iran's new Supreme Leader Mojtaba Khamenei has reportedly been flown to Moscow for emergency leg surgery after being wounded in US-Israeli strikes on Tehran. Iranian officials have confirmed he's wounded but given no further details. Kuwaiti media broke the story. Multiple sources cited by the Daily Mail, Report.az and Azerbaijani news outlets describe a Russian military aircraft evacuating him.

Meanwhile, a video of Netanyahu in a coffee shop — meant to prove he survived Iranian retaliation — has been flagged by Grok as potentially AI-generated. Israel says it's real. Iran's IRGC says it'll hunt Netanyahu if he's alive.

Neither claim can be independently verified. Both are shaping military decisions in real time. This is what happens when deepfakes meet active warfare: the truth becomes a weapon, and nobody can tell who's firing it.

## How the world sees this differently

Western media frames the Gulf strikes as Iranian aggression — a reckless widening of war against neutral states. The US-European narrative holds that Iran chose escalation, and coalition expansion is the logical response.

Middle Eastern media frames the same events as predictable consequences of a war Iran didn't start. Gulf states are angry at both sides: at Iran for hitting them, and at the US-Israeli coalition for creating the conditions that made it inevitable. The Guardian quoted Gulf residents describing the situation as their "worst nightmare" — a war they opposed, fought over their heads.

Asian media barely covers the military dynamics. It covers the economic fallout. Bloomberg's Asia desk isn't writing about missiles. It's writing about rice paddies, fuel pumps and crisis menus. For billions of people from Delhi to Manila, this isn't a geopolitical story. It's a survival story.

Pakistan's coverage, meanwhile, sits at the intersection of all three narratives. It's fighting its own war. It's economically devastated by someone else's. And its streets are erupting over both — ten people died when US Marines opened fire at the Karachi consulate during anti-war protests last week.

## What to watch tomorrow

Three things will determine whether this crisis deepens or stabilises.

First, Dubai airport. If flights resume normally, markets may briefly calm. If drone attacks continue hitting civilian infrastructure, insurance costs for Gulf shipping and aviation will spike further.

Second, Pakistan's harvest. Diesel availability in the next two weeks will determine whether Punjab's spring planting happens on schedule. If it doesn't, Pakistan faces a food crisis by autumn on top of everything else.

Third, the Khamenei question. If Iran's supreme leader is genuinely incapacitated, the IRGC's command structure becomes the de facto decision-maker. That changes the calculus for every negotiation attempt — because there may be nobody authorised to say yes.

The war started in Iran. It's now in Dubai, Riyadh, Bahrain, Kandahar, Karachi and every fuel pump from Mumbai to Manila. One crisis. One system. No off-ramp in sight.
