On Wednesday, Egyptian Prime Minister Mostafa Madbouly looked into a television camera and told 109 million people to stop driving unless they absolutely had to. "We must reduce journeys that are unnecessary so we don't reach the stage of an energy crisis," he said, before adding a line that no Egyptian politician uses lightly: "We will have to take more difficult measures, definitely more difficult."

In a country where bread prices have toppled governments, "more difficult measures" is not a throwaway phrase. It is a signal.

Five weeks into the Iran war, the economic shockwave has hit Egypt with a force that the pandemic, the Ukraine invasion, and the Gaza conflict did not. The Egyptian pound has fallen from 47 to the dollar to 54 since fighting began on February 28 — a 15 percent collapse in barely a month. Domestic fuel and gas prices rose 30 percent in March. Restaurants, cafés, cinemas, and malls now close at 9pm on weekdays. Government employees work from home one day a week, with a second day coming if the war continues.

And at the centre of all of it: wheat.

## The bread equation

Egypt is the world's largest wheat importer. About 70 million of its citizens — nearly two-thirds of the population — depend on a heavily subsidised flatbread called *eish balady* as their primary source of calories. Egyptians consume roughly 180 kilograms of wheat per person per year, among the highest rates on Earth.

The subsidy is not welfare in the Western sense. It is the load-bearing wall of Egyptian social stability. When Anwar Sadat tried to cut bread subsidies in 1977, riots erupted across the country within hours. When food prices spiked globally in 2010 and 2011, protesters in Tahrir Square chanted "bread, freedom, and social justice" — and Hosni Mubarak fell.

Every Egyptian government since has understood this equation: bread stays cheap, or the state is at risk.

The Iran war is now testing that equation from multiple directions simultaneously. Brent crude above $109 per barrel drives up the cost of everything from fertiliser to transport. The Hormuz Strait blockade has collapsed global shipping through the passage from over 130 crossings per day to single digits, rerouting supply chains and inflating freight costs. Egypt's Suez Canal revenues — already battered by Houthi attacks in 2024 — have taken another hit as global trade routes shift, costing the economy an estimated $10 billion according to the World Bank.

## Racing to stockpile

The government's response has been a sprint to secure reserves before the situation worsens. Prime Minister Madbouly said Wednesday that stocks of "strategic goods" — wheat, vegetable oils, corn, oil, and gas — were sufficient for six months, and that the government was working to add two to three months more.

Finance Minister Ahmed Kouchouk announced that the procurement price for locally grown wheat would rise from 2,225 to 2,500 Egyptian pounds per *ardeb* — a 150-kilogram measure — to incentivise farmers to sell to the government rather than private buyers. Egypt is targeting five million tonnes of domestic wheat procurement this harvest season, part of a long-term push to reduce its dependence on imports that historically reach 10 million tonnes per year.

The Italian Institute for International Political Studies (ISPI) reported in February that Egypt held strategic wheat reserves of approximately 4.5 million tonnes — enough for about four months. That figure represented a significant improvement from a decade ago, when reserves covered barely five weeks. But four months of reserves against a war with no visible end date is not comfortable.

Particularly when the country still sources the majority of its imported wheat from Russia and Ukraine. Russian wheat accounted for 55 percent of Egypt's imports in 2025, with Ukraine providing over 30 percent. The war in Iran has not yet disrupted Black Sea grain flows, but the cascading effects — higher fuel costs, shipping route disruptions, currency depreciation — have made every tonne of imported wheat more expensive.

## The invisible squeeze

What makes Egypt's crisis particularly invisible to English-language audiences is its slow-motion nature. There is no single dramatic event — no missile strike, no sinking ship. Instead, there is a grinding compression of daily life that accumulates week by week.

The pound falls. Fuel rises. A litre of cooking oil costs more than it did last month. The minibus fare goes up. The restaurant that once closed at midnight now shutters at nine. A government employee who drove to work five days a week now drives four, then perhaps three.

For the roughly 60 percent of Egyptians living near or below the poverty line, these increments are not inconveniences. They are the difference between three meals and two.

The Council on Foreign Relations flagged Egypt as "highly exposed" to the Iran war's food-system disruption due to its wheat import dependency. The UN Economic and Social Commission for Western Asia (ESCWA) warned this week that five million more Arabs face food insecurity from a 20 percent rise in regional food prices. Egypt, with 109 million people and a currency in freefall, absorbs a disproportionate share of that burden.

## What the history says

Egypt has navigated food crises before. The 2022 Ukraine shock prompted diversification efforts, expanded domestic cultivation through the massive Mustaqbal Misr agricultural project, and a modernisation of grain storage infrastructure. Those investments are now being tested under conditions more severe than the scenario they were designed for.

The prime minister's warning on Wednesday was notable not for what it announced, but for what it prepared the public to accept. Egyptian leaders do not discuss "harsher measures" unless they believe harsher measures may be necessary. The minimum wage increase of 1,000 pounds — to 8,000 pounds per month, roughly $148 — announced alongside the warning was a cushion, not a solution.

What happens next depends on time. If the Iran war ends within weeks, Egypt weathers a painful but manageable shock. If it extends through summer — when wheat reserves draw down, energy demand peaks, and the lean season arrives — the calculus changes.

The last time bread got political in Cairo, a government fell. Almost no one in the English-speaking world is watching this time.
