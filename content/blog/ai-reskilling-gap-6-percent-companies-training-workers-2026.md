Eighty-nine percent of business leaders say AI skills are critical. Only 6% have started training anyone. That's the defining disconnect of 2026.

The numbers come from a Metaintro analysis of workforce data, and they match a pattern across multiple reports this month. DataCamp found 59% of enterprise leaders admit their organisations have an AI skills gap — even as most claim to invest in training. The WEF estimates 59% of the global workforce will need reskilling by 2030. IMF managing director Kristalina Georgieva compared the shift to a "tsunami" hitting 40% of global jobs — 60% in advanced economies.

Everyone agrees the wave is coming. Almost nobody's teaching people to swim.

## The $300 Billion Contradiction

Companies are spending $300 billion on AI systems in 2026. They're buying the tools, building infrastructure, deploying models. They're not preparing the humans who have to use them.

A 2026 workforce analysis found 95% of AI pilot projects fail to reach scale. Not because the tech doesn't work — because organisations bolt AI onto existing workflows instead of redesigning how people do their jobs.

The typical corporate response? A webinar. Maybe an optional online course. A lunch-and-learn slide deck about "prompt engineering." That's not reskilling. It's checkbox compliance dressed up as transformation.

The OECD's 2025 Skills Outlook made this plain: only 19% of adults without upper-secondary education take part in non-formal learning, compared with 61% of university-educated workers. The people most at risk from AI are the least likely to get training. The gap's widening.

## Who Gets Trained — Who Gets Left Behind

The WEF's Future of Jobs report surveyed over 1,000 major employers. About 80% said they'd upskill workers with AI training. Two-thirds planned to hire for specific AI skills. Only 40% expected to cut headcount.

Encouraging — until you look at what "upskilling" means in practice. The WEF's most in-demand skills are analytical thinking, creativity, collaboration, digital literacy. These take months or years to build. A four-hour "Introduction to AI" course doesn't get anyone there.

Fast Company went further this week, asking a question most training plans avoid: can you retrain someone from a routine-task role into a high-judgment, AI-augmented position? Their answer was blunt. If filling "top 20%" jobs is hard with experienced candidates, it doesn't get easier by upskilling workers who've never done that kind of work.

This isn't about intelligence. It's about the canyon between a two-day training programme and what these new roles actually demand.

## The DoorDash Paradox

This week, DoorDash launched a "Tasks" app paying its 8 million couriers to film themselves doing household chores — loading dishwashers, folding laundry, opening doors — to generate training data for AI and robotics companies.

Gig workers paid to teach machines physical tasks. The same workers aren't learning new skills themselves. They're generating the data that trains the systems that may replace them.

DoorDash told Bloomberg the footage goes to "in-house AI models and those built by partners across retail, insurance, hospitality and technology sectors." Workers get paid per clip. The AI gets permanently smarter. The workers don't.

The reskilling gap in a single product. Investment flows to the machine. The human gets a per-task payment. Nobody pretends it's training.

## What This Means for Education

The pressure's flowing into classrooms and universities. The IMF called for redesigning education so students gain cognitive, creative, and technical skills that complement AI rather than compete with it. Online platforms report surging AI-related enrolments. Universities are testing AI tutors and adaptive learning.

But the OECD warns that without deliberate policy, benefits flow to the already well-educated. A university student with a laptop can learn prompt engineering in a weekend. A factory worker whose job just got automated needs something more structured, more supported, and more expensive than what most companies or governments offer.

The WEF named the skills gap the biggest barrier to business transformation. Nearly 40% of job skills will change by 2030. Sixty-three percent of employers call it their top challenge. This won't fix itself. It needs investment matching what companies pour into AI.

## The Real Question

The WEF projects a net gain of 78 million jobs by 2030 — 170 million created, 92 million displaced. That's possible. But it assumes workers displaced from vanishing roles can move into new ones. Right now, 94% of companies aren't trying to make that happen.

Executives know the tsunami's coming. AI hardware and software budgets are enormous. Budgets for teaching humans to work in an AI-transformed economy are, for most organisations, zero.

That 6% figure isn't a statistic. It's a policy failure in real time.
