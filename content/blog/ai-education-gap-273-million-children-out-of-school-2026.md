Three reports landed in the same week and together they tell a story nobody planned: AI is making education better for children who already have it, and irrelevant for the 273 million who don't.

UNESCO's 2026 Global Education Monitoring Report, released March 25, found that the number of children out of school has climbed for the seventh consecutive year. The same day, Anthropic published its fifth economic impact index showing AI tools concentrate their benefits in high-income countries among experienced users. And a Stanford review of 800-plus studies on [AI in classrooms](/blog/countries-racing-to-replace-teachers-with-ai-tutors-before-evidence-is-in) concluded that only 20 met rigorous causal standards — and that learning gains vanish when the AI is taken away.

These aren't contradictory findings. They're the same finding, viewed from different angles.

## The number keeps climbing

One in six school-age children worldwide is not in school. The 273 million figure — which covers children, adolescents, and youth — has risen 3% since 2015, reversing decades of progress. Sub-Saharan Africa has been hit hardest, mostly due to population growth outpacing school construction. Conflict zones push the real number even higher: UNESCO estimates an additional 13 million children in the ten most conflict-affected countries aren't captured in official statistics.

The Middle East has become a particular crisis point. Ongoing regional tensions — including the [Iran war that has disrupted education for 52 million children](/blog/iran-war-52-million-children-education-crisis-2026) — have forced schools to close across multiple countries, leaving millions at risk of never returning.

But here's what makes the 2026 data different from previous years: it arrives at the exact moment governments are betting billions on AI as the fix.

## The $55,000 AI classroom

Alpha Schools is opening its newest campus in Chicago this fall. The model: children learn core academics entirely from AI software for two hours each morning. No teachers — only "guides" who motivate and coach. The company has grown from 200 students to over 1,000 at 22 locations across the United States in two years, with plans to expand to 35.

Annual tuition: $55,000.

That price tag captures something the industry rarely says out loud. The most advanced AI education currently available — the version that replaces teachers entirely — costs roughly the same as a year at Stanford. The children UNESCO is counting, the ones in sub-Saharan Africa and conflict zones and rural South Asia, aren't just priced out of Alpha Schools. Many don't have electricity.

UNESCO's own data makes this concrete. Electrification alone has been linked to nearly a full extra year of schooling in Cambodia. School feeding programs add up to half a year of learning for every $100 spent in low- and middle-income countries. The basics still matter more than the algorithms.

## What Stanford actually found

The Stanford AI Hub for Education reviewed more than 800 academic papers on AI in K-12 classrooms. Out of those 800-plus, only about 20 were high-quality causal studies — the kind that can actually prove whether AI itself drives better outcomes, rather than factors like teacher quality or student motivation.

The results within that small pool were specific and limited. AI tools helped students improve on structured tasks — math practice problems, writing feedback. Teachers saved up to 30% of their time on grading and lesson planning without lowering quality.

But here's the part that should concern anyone planning a national AI education rollout: when the AI was removed, the gains disappeared.

"It's still too early to know if this is doing school faster or if it's reimagining," said Chris Agnew, managing director of Stanford's AI Hub for Education. "The headline is mixed."

Lily Fesler, a senior researcher on the team, added that without causal evidence, improvements could be driven by teacher experience or classroom context rather than the tool itself. Most existing research reflects simple chatbot-style products, not how AI is actually being used in real classrooms.

This matters because at least ten countries — including El Salvador, Kazakhstan, Estonia, and the UAE — are already deploying AI tutoring at national scale. They're running ahead of the evidence.

## The Anthropic paradox

The most striking data point this week came from the company that should be most optimistic about AI's potential.

Anthropic's fifth economic impact report, released March 25, analyzed how its Claude AI is used across the economy. The findings on education are quietly damning for the "AI as equalizer" narrative.

First: AI tools are used far more intensely in high-income countries. The top 20 countries account for 48% of all per-capita usage, up from 45% in the previous report. Global inequality in AI adoption is growing, not shrinking.

Second: experienced users get dramatically more value. People who have used Claude for six months or more have a 10% higher success rate in their conversations — and this gap isn't explained by what tasks they choose or where they live. They've learned how to use AI effectively, and that learning itself is a skill that compounds over time.

Third: as adoption spreads to new users, the average economic value of work done on AI platforms is actually decreasing. New users tend to try lower-value tasks. The power users pull further ahead.

Anthropic's own head of economics, Peter McCrory, said the pattern means "displacement effects could materialize very quickly." His recommendation: build monitoring systems now, before the gap becomes permanent.

For education, the implication is clear. AI tutoring works better the more you already know how to use AI. Children in well-resourced schools with trained teachers and reliable internet will get better at using these tools. Children without those foundations won't just miss out — they'll fall further behind relative to their peers than they would have without AI.

## The evidence we're missing

Here's what nobody has published yet: results from a national-scale AI tutoring deployment in a developing country.

El Salvador handed its entire public education system to xAI's Grok platform — one million students across 5,000 schools. OpenAI's "Education for Countries" program is running in eight nations. Google announced plans to [train six million teachers on AI tools](/blog/google-ai-training-6-million-teachers-shortage-crisis-march-2026). These are real experiments running right now.

But none have released outcome data. Not one. The LLMs-in-education market is projected to grow from $7.5 billion in 2026 to over $35 billion by 2030, according to a March 2026 GlobeNewsWire report. Billions flowing in, zero causal evidence flowing out.

Meanwhile, the AFT launched its National Academy for AI Instruction on March 18 in New York, training 400,000 teachers on AI classroom use. It's one of the few programs acknowledging that the technology doesn't work without the teacher. A Jobs for the Future survey found that 40% of learners cite the high cost of premium AI tools as a major barrier — a number that approaches 100% in the countries where UNESCO's 273 million live.

## What would actually help

The UNESCO report buries the most useful finding in its policy section. In 14 African countries, making education compulsory (not just free) added over a year of schooling for boys and girls alike. When child labor laws were added, gains grew further. Cash transfers tied to school attendance made children 36% more likely to be enrolled.

These are boring interventions. Nobody gives a keynote at Davos about school feeding programs. But they have causal evidence — the same standard that 98% of AI education studies fail to meet.

The question isn't whether AI can help education. It probably can, in specific contexts, for specific tasks, with trained teachers and working infrastructure. The question is whether the current rush to deploy AI is pulling attention and funding away from interventions that work right now for the children who need them most.

Two hundred and seventy-three million children aren't waiting for a better chatbot. They're waiting for a school.
