Yemen's Houthis fired two waves of missiles and drones at Israel on Saturday, entering the Iran war for the first time. The same day, Maersk halted operations at Oman's Salalah port. Together, these events threaten the Saudi pipeline-to-Red-Sea workaround that's been keeping 7 million barrels of oil per day flowing while the [Strait of Hormuz remains closed](/blog/hormuz-strait-closed-oil-shipping-food-crisis-march-2026). Both of the world's critical oil chokepoints are now under active threat — and there's no Plan C.

Saudi Arabia spent 40 years building its fallback. On Saturday, the Houthis put it in the crosshairs.

## The Lifeline That Just Got a Bullseye

Since the [Hormuz closure](/blog/houthis-join-iran-war-beersheba-missile-bab-al-mandeb-2026) began a month ago, Saudi Arabia has been running its East-West pipeline at full capacity — 7 million barrels per day, pumped 1,000 kilometres across the Arabian Peninsula to the Red Sea port of Yanbu. About 5 million barrels a day are being exported from Yanbu, with another 700,000–900,000 barrels of refined products. It's the reason oil sits at $112 instead of $150.

That oil leaves Yanbu, sails south through the Red Sea, passes through the Bab el-Mandeb strait — a 29-kilometre gap between Yemen and Djibouti — and heads to global markets.

The Houthis control the Yemeni coastline along that gap.

## Two Waves, One Message

Saturday's attacks came in two distinct salvos. First, a ballistic missile triggered air raid sirens across Beersheba in southern Israel. The IDF confirmed interception. Hours later, Houthi military spokesperson Yahya Saree announced a second wave: "a barrage of cruise missiles and drones" targeting what he described as sensitive Israeli military sites.

Israel intercepted both strikes. But the military significance matters less than the signal. "We are preparing for a multifront war," Israeli military spokesperson Brig. Gen. Effie Defrin told reporters.

The Houthis' message wasn't aimed at Israel alone. "Our fingers are on the trigger," Saree had warned the day before. "We will not allow the US and Israel to use the Red Sea for attacks on Iran."

## Maersk Blinks

The timing of Maersk's Salalah port halt tells the story. The world's second-largest container shipping company suspended operations the same day, citing a "security incident." The port — located in southern Oman, along the Arabian Sea route connecting the Gulf to the Red Sea — is a critical transshipment hub. Maersk estimated a 48-hour shutdown.

Salalah sits at the junction between the safe and the dangerous. Ships loading Saudi oil at Yanbu must transit back through Houthi-threatened waters. Container vessels heading for Europe through Suez pass through the same corridor. If shipping companies won't call at ports near the conflict zone, the reroute around the Cape of Good Hope adds 10–14 days and costs that eventually land on grocery shelves.

## The Math Gets Brutal

Consider what moves through these two chokepoints. The Strait of Hormuz once carried 20% of the world's crude. The Red Sea and Bab el-Mandeb handle 12–15% of all global maritime trade, including 30% of container shipping, 8% of global grain trade, and 8% of liquefied natural gas.

"If Hormuz is hit, oil struggles to leave the Gulf. If Bab el-Mandeb is blocked, it struggles to reach Europe. If both are hit, the route breaks end to end," The National reported this week.

That end-to-end break is no longer hypothetical. It's the scenario markets are now pricing.

Insurance war risk premiums for vessels transiting the Red Sea jumped from 0.25–0.5% of vessel value per trip to 5% or more during the 2023–2024 Houthi campaign. Those premiums had started dropping after the Gaza peace plan paused Houthi attacks in October 2025. Now they'll climb again — and this time, with Hormuz already closed, there's nowhere else to go.

## The Framing Gap

How you understand Saturday depends on where you read about it.

AP reported the attacks through a shipping-risk lens: "A missile attack on Israel by Iranian-backed Houthi rebels raises concern that Tehran's proxies may again try to block Red Sea shipping routes." The emphasis is on what the Houthis might do to trade.

Arabic media told a different story entirely. Al Jazeera described it as Ansar Allah "joining the battle" — military analysts framing it as "a dual strategy: unity of fronts to support Iran, and securing a negotiating position." Ultra Palestine called it "the first attack since the start of *the aggression* on Iran." The word aggression — عدوان — dominates Arabic coverage and doesn't appear in English reporting.

The Perception Gap Index scored Houthi coverage at 7.1 this scan, with the US-Middle East divergence hitting 8.0. Same missiles. Same day. Two different wars.

## What's Actually at Stake

Forget the missiles for a moment. Think about what 7 million barrels a day through Yanbu actually means for ordinary people.

That oil becomes the [fertilizer that grows next year's food](/blog/fertilizer-crisis-hormuz-blockade-spring-planting-2026). It powers the ships that carry grain from Brazil to Egypt. It heats homes in Japan, where the energy bill just passed ¥15 trillion. It fuels the trucks delivering rice across Southeast Asia, where [700 million people already face an energy crisis](/blog/southeast-asia-rice-harvest-fuel-crisis-food-2026).

Saudi Arabia spent decades building the Yanbu pipeline precisely for a moment like this — a contingency born from the 1980s Iran-Iraq war, when ships came under fire in the Strait. Now the contingency is working, and the group that spent two years proving it can shut down Red Sea shipping just announced it's back.

"It would be devastating for so many countries," said Ahmed Nagi, senior Yemen analyst at the International Crisis Group. "If we see more pressure on the Iranians, or there's any escalation, the Houthis will jump in harshly."

## No Plan C

The Western track record of protecting shipping routes from the Houthis is not encouraging. Reuters reported this week that a similar effort in the Red Sea "started years earlier, cost billions of dollars, and ultimately failed." The US-led Operation Prosperity Guardian couldn't stop Houthi attacks in 2024. The Houthis don't need to sink a tanker. They just need to make the route expensive enough that insurers won't cover it.

Right now, the world's oil is flowing through a single workaround that passes directly under the guns of a militia that just declared war. The pipeline works. The port works. The question is whether the 29-kilometre gap at the end of the route stays open.

One month ago, the world had two maritime oil corridors. Today, both are contested. The alternative to the alternative doesn't exist.
