The Iran war's food crisis just doubled. Russia halted ammonium nitrate exports on March 24 — cutting 40% of global trade in the fertilizer — the same week Yemen's Houthis threatened to block Bab al-Mandab, the Red Sea chokepoint that handles 12% of global shipping. With the [Strait of Hormuz already under an Iranian blockade](/blog/hormuz-strait-closed-oil-shipping-food-crisis-march-2026) for 28 days, the Northern Hemisphere's spring planting season is now caught between two blockades and a deliberate supply cut. Fertilizer plants are shutting down on three continents. The war's next casualties won't wear uniforms.

The NYT reported on March 27 that cascading effects on fertilizer supply are "growing worse by the day." CNBC called it a food security warning. China's state media led with the humanitarian angle. Most Western coverage buried the fertilizer story below oil prices and market data. The perception gap is widening between those who see this as a [food and energy crisis hitting hundreds of millions](/blog/318-million-hunger-famine-fertilizer-crisis-iran-war-2026) and those who see it as a stock market correction.

## The Double Squeeze on Food

Nitrogen and phosphate — the two fertilizer nutrients that determine whether crops grow or fail — now face simultaneous disruption from opposite sides of the globe.

The Hormuz blockade has choked off Gulf fertilizer exports since Day 1 of the war. Qatar, Saudi Arabia, and the UAE together supply roughly 25% of the world's traded nitrogen and phosphate. Only 28 ships passed through Hormuz in the past seven days. The normal rate is 138 per day.

Russia's move is separate but devastatingly timed. Moscow announced on March 24 that it would halt ammonium nitrate exports until April 21 to protect its own planting season. Russia controls up to 40% of global ammonium nitrate trade. The ban covers exactly the weeks when farmers from Iowa to India need to apply nitrogen to spring crops.

India has shut fertilizer plants dependent on imported natural gas. Algeria and Slovakia have done the same. Without nitrogen application, corn and wheat yields can drop by 50%, according to the Financial Content analysis of the 2026 planting season.

## The Third Chokepoint Nobody's Discussing

On March 27, Houthi military spokesman Yahya Saree announced that Yemen's forces have their "fingers on the trigger for direct military intervention." He outlined three red lines: the Red Sea being used for operations against Iran, other countries joining the anti-Iran coalition, and further escalation.

This isn't hypothetical posturing. The Houthis control Yemen's coastline along Bab al-Mandab, the narrow strait connecting the Red Sea to the Gulf of Aden. If they act, two of the world's three most critical oil chokepoints close simultaneously.

Here's what makes this worse: the main bypass for Hormuz is Saudi Arabia's East-West pipeline, which moves oil to Yanbu on the Red Sea coast. If Houthis threaten the Red Sea, the bypass itself becomes vulnerable. Hormuz carries 21 million barrels per day in normal times. The combined capacity of all bypass routes covers maybe 5 million. The math doesn't work.

The Stimson Center warned that Houthi action in Bab al-Mandab would "deepen supply chain disruptions, raise insurance and logistics costs, and extend a de facto blockade from Hormuz to Suez." Saudi Arabia, already hit by Iranian missiles at Prince Sultan Air Base that wounded 12 US troops on March 27, now faces potential threats from both its northern border and its southern one.

## Israel Expands to Industry, Iran Expands to Nukes

While the food crisis builds quietly, the shooting war escalated on two fronts Friday.

Israel struck Khuzestan Steel near Ahvaz and Mobarakeh Steel in Isfahan — Iran's two largest steel producers. One person died and 15 were injured at the Isfahan complex. The IDF warned beforehand of an "escalate and expand" campaign. The NYT headlined it: "Strikes on Iranian Industry Expand Blows to Civilian Economy." Al Jazeera framed the same strikes as targeting "civilian livelihoods." Israel calls the plants "IRGC-linked." Iran calls them foundational to its civilian economy. Both descriptions can be true.

The steel strikes represent a new category of targeting. For 27 days, the air campaign focused on military installations, naval assets, and nuclear facilities. Hitting steel plants signals a shift toward economic destruction — degrading Iran's ability to rebuild, not just its ability to fight.

Iran responded by breaching US air defences at Prince Sultan Air Base in Saudi Arabia with a combined missile and drone attack. Twelve American troops were wounded, two seriously. It was the most direct hit on US forces since the war began. Iran also sent a cluster missile into Tel Aviv, killing a 52-year-old man and hitting more than ten sites.

Meanwhile, Reuters reported that Iranian hardliners are intensifying calls for a nuclear weapon. The debate is "louder, more public and more insistent," sources inside Iran told Reuters on March 26. The ISW confirmed hardliners are urging leadership to revise Iran's nuclear doctrine. The war launched partly to prevent a nuclear Iran may be accelerating the very outcome it was designed to stop.

## Pakistan: Fighting One War, Mediating Another

Pakistan's Foreign Minister Ishaq Dar confirmed on March 26 that indirect US-Iran talks are running through Islamabad. A 15-point American proposal is "being deliberated" by Tehran. Pakistan has called Washington, Tehran, Riyadh, Abu Dhabi, Cairo, Istanbul, and Brussels — an extraordinary burst of diplomatic activity from a country simultaneously [bombing Afghanistan](/blog/afghanistan-strikes-pakistan-bases-nur-khan-rawalpindi-march-2026).

The paradox is impossible to overstate. Pakistan resumed military operations in Afghanistan after Eid al-Fitr. Human Rights Watch declared Pakistan's March 16 airstrike on a Kabul addiction treatment hospital a "possible war crime." Pakistan ranks first on the 2026 Global Terrorism Index, with the TTP conducting 595 attacks last year. Petrol has crossed Rs 321 per litre and is still climbing.

And yet Pakistan is the only country talking to both sides. Reuters noted that Vance, Witkoff, and Kushner are expected to engage with Pakistani counterparts. India's media mocks it as a "joke." But the phone lines run through Islamabad because no one else's can.

Pakistan needs both wars to end. The Hormuz blockade is crushing its oil import bill — PIDE estimates an extra $4.5 billion at $160 per barrel. The Afghanistan border closure has cut trade corridors. The Iran border is disrupted. Pakistan's economy is being squeezed from every direction. Mediation isn't altruism. It's survival.

## How the World Sees Day 28

The framing gap between regions hasn't been this wide since the war began.

US and European media led Friday with the Dow entering correction — down more than 10% from its February peak, the fifth straight losing week. Brent crude closed at $112.57, up 53% from pre-war levels. The S&P 500 fell 1.7%. For American audiences, the war is now about retirement accounts and gas prices.

Al Jazeera and Middle Eastern outlets led with the Prince Sultan base attack and Israel's industrial strikes. The framing is consistently "US-Israeli war on Iran" — placing agency with the attackers. Iran's death toll, tracked at 1,937 by Al Jazeera (likely understated — the independent HRANA counted 3,114 by March 17), remains a lead story.

Chinese state media, particularly Xinhua, led with the fertilizer and food supply angle. The NYT's own reporting on the global food bottleneck ran well below its market coverage. Beijing treats the planting crisis as a food emergency. Washington treats it as a commodity price story. The difference matters: one framing demands humanitarian intervention, the other demands monetary policy.

Indian media remained fixated on mocking Pakistan's mediation bid while quietly noting that India secured selective Hormuz transit from Iran — a strategic advantage it doesn't want to discuss loudly.

## What April 6 Means for Your Kitchen Table

Trump extended his deadline for Iran to reopen Hormuz to April 6. If Iran doesn't comply, the US has threatened to strike Iran's power grid. Iran has promised, in response, to destroy Gulf desalination plants, mine the entire Persian Gulf, and permanently close Hormuz.

If that escalation happens, the fertilizer crisis becomes a famine timeline. Gulf desalination plants provide drinking water for 30 million people. Mining the Persian Gulf would halt bypass pipeline loading terminals. And the Houthi threat means even the Red Sea alternative could be gone.

The Northern Hemisphere's planting window for wheat and corn closes in roughly six weeks. Farmers who can't get fertilizer by mid-April won't get it at all this season. The crop that doesn't get planted in April doesn't arrive on supermarket shelves in September.

Two straits. One fertilizer ban. One planting season. The war that started with missiles is becoming a war on food — and most of the world's media is still counting barrels, not bushels.
