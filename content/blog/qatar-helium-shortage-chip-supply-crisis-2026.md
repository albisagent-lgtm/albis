The gas that fills party balloons is about to determine whether you can buy a new laptop this year.

When Iranian drones struck Qatar's Ras Laffan facility on March 2, the immediate headlines were about liquefied natural gas. What almost nobody noticed: the world lost 30% of its helium supply overnight.

Three weeks later, the clock is ticking. Helium spot prices have surged up to 100% since the crisis began, according to Phil Kornbluth, president of Kornbluth Helium Consulting. Containers that were filled at Ras Laffan before the strike are still in transit, buying the semiconductor industry a few more weeks. After that, the shortage hits.

"Nobody's run out of helium yet," Kornbluth told Fortune. "But it's a few weeks out when the shortage really hits."

## Why Helium Is Irreplaceable

Here's what makes this different from a typical supply disruption: there is no substitute.

During semiconductor manufacturing, chip fabs blow helium gas over the back of silicon wafers to draw heat away during the etching process — the step where transistor structures are carved into the silicon. Helium's thermal conductivity makes it uniquely suited for maintaining the precise temperatures that advanced chipmaking demands.

"Under current semiconductor manufacturing processes, there's no viable replacement for helium to cool wafers," said Jong-hwan Lee, a professor of semiconductor devices at South Korea's Sangmyung University.

The same gas cools the superconducting magnets inside every MRI machine in every hospital. It purges fuel tanks for SpaceX and Blue Origin rocket launches. It enables the quantum computing research that companies are betting billions on. And one country was producing nearly a third of the global supply.

## South Korea Is Most Exposed

South Korea imported 64.7% of its helium from Qatar in 2025, according to the Korea International Trade Association. That's a problem when your country is home to Samsung Electronics and SK Hynix — the world's two largest memory chip makers, responsible for roughly 18% of global semiconductor production capacity.

Fitch Ratings flagged South Korea as "particularly vulnerable" to a prolonged helium shortage. Seoul's Ministry of Trade has launched emergency monitoring of 14 strategic materials. SK Hynix says it has diversified supplies and secured sufficient inventory. Samsung has activated helium recycling systems on some production lines, with current technology recovering 60–80% of helium used.

TSMC, which fabricates the most advanced AI chips for Nvidia, Apple, and others, says it doesn't "currently anticipate a notable impact" — but is monitoring the situation closely.

The caution in those statements tells you everything. Nobody is panicking yet. Everyone is preparing to.

## The Two-Week Clock

The math is stark. Qatar's state-owned QatarGas declared force majeure on March 4 and has since reported "extensive" damage from follow-up Iranian strikes that hit Ras Laffan again last week. Reuters reported that helium exports will drop 14%, and the facility could take years to fully repair.

Tom's Hardware called it a "two-week clock" — the window before existing helium inventories run dry at major fabs. Korean chipmakers reportedly hold about six months of stockpiles, but that assumes no acceleration in drawdown and no disruption to alternative suppliers.

The situation echoes the 2022 shortage of neon gas triggered by Russia's invasion of Ukraine, which disrupted semiconductor lithography. The chip industry diversified after that scare. But helium is harder to replace, and the concentration risk was even more extreme — one facility, one country, one-third of global output.

## What Breaks Next

If the shortage extends beyond weeks into months, the cascade reaches consumers. HP, Dell, Lenovo, Acer, and ASUS have already warned enterprise clients of potential 15–20% price hikes heading into the second half of 2026.

The AI boom makes the timing brutal. Every major tech company is racing to build data centres packed with advanced chips that require helium to manufacture. A prolonged shortage doesn't just slow production — it forces chipmakers to choose which chips to prioritise, which customers to serve first, and which product lines to cut.

In a shortage, industry protocol prioritises medical imaging, semiconductors, and aerospace first. Party balloons and industrial welding go to the back of the queue. But even with triage, there simply may not be enough to go around.

## The Invisible Chain

This is what makes the Hormuz crisis so much larger than oil prices. The same war that is driving Brent crude past $112 a barrel and threatening water for 100 million Gulf residents is also quietly putting a chokehold on the materials that power the global technology economy.

Iran's desalination threats scored a Perception Gap Index of 7.68 out of 10 across Albis scans this week — Gulf media treated it as existential while Western media buried it under energy coverage. The helium story follows the same pattern: the most consequential supply chain disruption is happening in plain sight, and most of the world hasn't connected the dots yet.

One drone strike. One gas facility. Thirty percent of a gas with no substitute. The party balloons will be fine. It's everything else that's the problem.
