Pakistani mortar rounds hit Kunar province on Saturday, killing a civilian and injuring a woman during what was supposed to be an Eid ceasefire. By Sunday, Ariana News reported a second violation in Paktika. The five-day truce, brokered by Saudi Arabia, Qatar, and Turkey, expires at midnight Monday — and neither side is talking about extending it.

The ceasefire was announced March 18 after weeks of relentless Pakistani airstrikes that displaced 115,000 Afghans and killed at least 75 civilians, according to the New York Times. The Omid Hospital strike in Kabul on March 16 — where UN officials say "several hundred" were killed and injured — had pushed the conflict past a line that even the Taliban's few remaining backers in Islamabad could not ignore. Saudi Crown Prince Mohammed bin Salman personally called Pakistan's army chief to press for a pause. The TTP, the Pakistani Taliban that operates across both countries, declared its own three-day Eid ceasefire hours later.

None of it held.

## A Ceasefire That Existed on Paper

Taliban spokesman Fasihuddin Fitrat accused Pakistan of "deception," saying the mortar attacks in eastern Afghanistan demonstrated that Islamabad never intended to honour the truce. Pakistan's military has not commented on the specific incidents, though its information minister maintained last week that the ceasefire was "comprehensive and unconditional."

The pattern is familiar. Pakistan's 2024 cross-border strikes into Afghanistan, codenamed "Operation Azm-e-Istehkam," followed a similar cycle: escalation, international pressure, temporary pause, resumption. But the 2026 conflict has crossed thresholds that make the old pattern harder to repeat. Pakistani jets bombed a hospital in a foreign capital. The US intelligence community, in its annual threat assessment, named Pakistan's nuclear arsenal as a risk for the first time — placing it alongside Russia and China.

Inside Pakistan, the TTP is exploiting every gap. Three police officers were killed Sunday in a TTP-linked attack on a police compound in Khyber Pakhtunkhwa, the province that borders Afghanistan's eastern flank. The TTP's own ceasefire, announced with theatrical piety about Eid observance, appears to have been selective at best. The group has carried out more than 400 attacks inside Pakistan since January, according to tracking data from the Pak Institute for Peace Studies.

## The Energy Noose Tightens

What makes Monday's ceasefire expiry different from previous cycles is the resource math. Pakistan's government warned citizens on March 19 to conserve fuel, acknowledging that the country's oil reserves sit at roughly 20 days of supply. The Hormuz Strait crisis — now in its fourth week — has choked the shipping lanes that Pakistan depends on for 85% of its crude imports.

Every day of fighting burns fuel Pakistan does not have. The Pakistani Air Force's F-16s and JF-17s consume thousands of gallons per sortie. The army's logistics chain across the rugged Afghan-Pakistan border relies on diesel-powered convoys climbing 10,000-foot passes. None of this is sustainable when the country's energy minister is telling refiners to ration.

The connection to the Iran war is direct and compounding. Pakistan finds itself in a two-front squeeze that no one in Islamabad planned for. To the west, its border with Iran — once a quiet stretch of Baloch desert — is now the perimeter of an active war zone. Iranian missiles have struck targets across the Gulf, and the IRGC has threatened to close Hormuz permanently if the US hits Iran's power plants. Trump's 48-hour ultimatum on that question expires around the same time as the Afghanistan ceasefire.

To the east, India watches with growing alarm. Former Pakistani ambassador Abdul Basit's threat to "bomb Delhi and Mumbai" — dismissed as bluster by Hindi media but consumed wall-to-wall across India — reflects a siege mentality in Pakistan's strategic class. The 2026 Global Terrorism Index ranked Pakistan as the most terrorism-affected country on earth. The US threat assessment named its nuclear weapons as a risk. The Hormuz crisis is draining its reserves. And the war it started in Afghanistan is producing body counts that make international support increasingly difficult to justify.

## The Mediators Have Left the Room

The deeper problem is diplomatic. Saudi Arabia, Qatar, and Turkey — the three countries that brokered the Eid ceasefire — are now fully consumed by the Iran crisis. Saudi Arabia expelled Iranian diplomats over the weekend, citing violations of the Beijing Agreement. Qatar is hosting back-channel talks on Hormuz navigation rights. Turkey's cabinet meets Monday to assess the economic fallout of oil at $112 per barrel.

None of them have bandwidth for Pakistan and Afghanistan right now.

The Financial Times published an editorial Sunday arguing that "the world needs to re-engage with the troubled region." But the world's diplomatic infrastructure is stretched across multiple crises running simultaneously. The same mediators cannot broker a ceasefire in South Asia, manage a Hormuz navigation dispute, and prevent an Iran-US war from going nuclear — all in the same week. Something gives.

What gives, historically, is the crisis with the fewest cameras pointed at it. Afghanistan has been that crisis before. It is becoming that crisis again.

## What Happens Tuesday

If the ceasefire expires without extension — and no talks are underway to extend it — Pakistan has signalled it will resume airstrikes. The Afghan Taliban has positioned forces along the border and warned of "consequences" for further incursions. Both sides claim they want peace. Neither has offered a mechanism to achieve it.

The Guardian reported Sunday that families in Kabul are still searching for loved ones in the rubble of Omid Hospital. The UN's Georgette Gagnon said the death toll there continues to rise. Across the border in Khyber Pakhtunkhwa, three more police families are burying their dead.

Monday brings two simultaneous deadlines: Trump's ultimatum to Iran and Pakistan's ceasefire with Afghanistan. Both expire in darkness. Both were supposed to create space for negotiation. Both, as of Sunday evening, appear to have created space only for the next escalation.
