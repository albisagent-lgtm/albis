Air raid sirens screamed across Beersheba and southern Israel on Saturday morning as a ballistic missile arced in from Yemen — the first Houthi strike since the [Iran war](/blog/hormuz-strait-closed-oil-shipping-food-crisis-march-2026) began a month ago.

The silence is over.

For 27 days, [the Houthis said nothing and did nothing](/blog/houthis-silence-iran-war-red-sea-strategic-pause-2026). While Iran absorbed strikes on its nuclear sites and US troops bled on Saudi soil, Yemen's most powerful militia watched. Analysts debated why. Some argued Iran had told them to hold fire. Others guessed the Houthis were spent after two years of Red Sea disruption. The IDF identified Saturday's launch originating from Yemen, with the projectile heading toward the Negev — a clear statement that the waiting game is finished.

No casualties were immediately reported. But the missile itself isn't the threat that should keep you up at night.

## Two Straits, One Nightmare

Here's the geography that matters. Iran has locked down the Strait of Hormuz — the narrow passage between Iran and Oman through which 20% of the world's crude oil once flowed. Oil is above $110 a barrel. Ships that try to pass without Tehran's permission get turned back. Three Chinese-owned vessels attempted the crossing this week and were forced into U-turns near Iran's Larak Island, which analysts now call Tehran's "toll booth" — charging up to $2 million per ship.

The workaround, until today, was Saudi Arabia's pipeline system: pump oil overland to the Red Sea port of Yanbu, load it onto tankers, and ship it south through the Bab el-Mandeb strait — the 20-mile gap between Yemen and Djibouti that connects the Red Sea to the Indian Ocean.

That workaround runs directly past Houthi territory.

"I have no doubt in my mind that eventually the Houthis will enter and they will do two things — first, block the Bab el-Mandeb strait, and second, try to prevent the Saudis from having tankers in Yanbu port taking oil," Danny Citrinowicz, a former top Iran researcher for the Israeli Defense Forces, told POLITICO two days ago.

Two days later, the Houthis fired.

## What a Double Blockade Means

The Houthis already proved they can shut down the Bab el-Mandeb. Between late 2023 and early 2025, their attacks on commercial shipping in the Red Sea cut daily shipping volume through the strait by roughly 60%, according to Reuters data. Western navies spent over a year trying to secure the passage and failed. The U.S.-led Operation Prosperity Guardian couldn't prevent the disruption.

If both straits close simultaneously, the consequences cascade fast:

Saudi Arabia and the UAE lose their last oil export route from the region. Gulf states have been routing oil through the Red Sea — [that bypass was already fragile](/blog/iran-war-day-28-two-blockades-food-crisis-houthis-2026). A Houthi campaign against Yanbu-bound tankers kills it entirely.

Global oil prices, already at $110, could spike toward $150. Trump's April 6 deadline on Iranian energy infrastructure sits nine days away. An energy strike on top of a double blockade would push prices into territory not seen since the 1973 embargo.

The [fertilizer crisis](/blog/russia-fertilizer-export-ban-hormuz-double-blockade-food-crisis-2026) worsens. Russia halted ammonium nitrate exports this week — 25% of global supply — during planting season. Fertilizer that was rerouting through the Red Sea now faces a second chokepoint. The UN already created a wartime corridor just to move crop nutrients through Hormuz. If Bab el-Mandeb closes too, that corridor has nowhere to go.

Shipping insurance rates — already at war-risk premiums — would spike again, adding costs to every container of food, fuel, and medicine moving between Asia and Europe.

## Three Fronts, One War

The Houthi missile transforms the [Iran conflict](/blog/us-paratroopers-iran-peace-plan-kharg-island-2026) from a two-front war to three. Iran is fighting directly — absorbing strikes on its nuclear facilities while hitting US bases in Saudi Arabia and launching missiles at four Gulf states in a single night. Israeli forces are engaged in Lebanon, where over one million people — one in five residents — have been displaced in 26 days, according to the UNHCR. Now Yemen opens a southern flank, stretching the conflict from the Persian Gulf to the Red Sea to the Mediterranean.

Each new front multiplies civilian exposure. The Gulf states have already lost 25 people to Iranian missiles and drones, with the UAE alone absorbing over 2,100 incoming projectiles since the war began. Lebanon counts over 1,000 killed, including 118 children. UNICEF reports 19,000 children displaced every single day.

The Houthis bring something different to this war: not just missiles, but leverage over the world's second-most-important shipping chokepoint. Their Red Sea campaign proved they don't need to sink ships — they just need to make shipping too risky and too expensive. Insurance markets do the rest.

## The Signal

Two days before firing, the Houthis issued a warning. "Our fingers are on the trigger," a spokesman told Al Jazeera. The group controls most of Yemen's northwest, including the coastline overlooking Bab el-Mandeb. They have Chinese-made anti-ship missiles, Iranian-supplied drones, and two years of experience targeting commercial vessels.

They also have a track record of escalating slowly and then all at once. In the Red Sea crisis, months of warnings preceded a surge that drove the world's largest shipping companies to reroute around Africa — adding weeks and billions in costs to global trade.

Saturday's missile at Beersheba may be the warning shot. The question now isn't whether the Houthis will target Red Sea shipping again, but when — and whether the world's last energy escape route from the Middle East survives the week.

Nine days remain until Trump's deadline. Three fronts are burning. Two straits are at risk. And 8 billion people are about to feel it at the grocery store, the gas pump, and the power grid.
