China's new five-year plan commits at least 7% more R&D spending annually to AI, quantum computing, semiconductors, biotech, and 6G. The same document allows emissions to rise 3-6% over five years. Two policies. One strategy: growth and independence trump any climate pledge you've heard Beijing make.

Here's the unified logic Western coverage misses: When the US chokes your chip supply, you choose. Decarbonize or build your own tech stack? China's picking the stack. The 15th Five-Year Plan says it twice — once in R&D billions, once in carbon budgets — but nobody's reading them together.

## The Tech Side: Billions to Break Free

China's pouring money into tech self-sufficiency: quantum computers, space-earth quantum networks, embodied AI for humanoid robots, machine-brain interfaces, 6G, advanced semiconductors. Nature reports at least 7% annual R&D growth, translating to billions more each year. Reuters confirms the plan targets "technological self-reliance" across AI and quantum.

This isn't innovation for innovation's sake. It's a forced march. US export controls on chips and advanced tech left China with a choice: import cutting-edge tools or build them domestically. The five-year plan chose build.

CSIS notes the restrictions "prompted China to implement an all-out, government-backed effort to improve the country's self-sufficiency in all aspects of semiconductor design and production." That effort's now baked into national policy. In March 2025, Beijing launched a $138 billion venture fund targeting quantum, AI, semiconductors, and renewables. The government isn't waiting for markets to solve this. It's writing checks.

The tech list is intentional. Quantum for secure communication. AI for everything. Semiconductors to escape dependence. Biotech for health and agriculture. 6G to own the next telecom standard. Every item points at a future where China doesn't need Western tech exports.

## The Climate Side: Growth First, Peak Later

The same document that funds this tech push allows emissions to rise. The 17% carbon intensity target lets absolute CO2 emissions grow 3-6% over five years if GDP hits 4.5-5% growth. Carbon Brief's Lauri Myllyvirta called it "alarmingly lax." The Centre for Research on Energy and Clean Air confirms: emissions can climb while still meeting the target.

China's committed to peak emissions before 2030 and carbon neutrality by 2060. The intensity target supports those goals — technically. Cut emissions per unit of GDP by 17%, and you're improving efficiency. But if GDP grows faster, total emissions still rise.

The plan doesn't set an emissions cap. It tracks intensity, not volume. That's deliberate. China's building industrial capacity — solar panels, batteries, EVs, steel, cement — and that takes energy. Lots of it. Decarbonizing while expanding production at this scale is nearly impossible. So China's choosing expansion.

Climate Action Tracker warns that hitting the 2030 NDC target (65% carbon intensity reduction from 2005) will require emissions to stay below 14.4 GtCO2e. That's lower than even optimistic projections. Translation: the current plan doesn't get China to its own 2030 climate promise without much harder cuts later.

## Why This Is One Strategy, Not Two

Here's what Western media treats as separate: tech ambitions, climate compromises. Read them together and you see a single decision.

US export controls backed China into a corner. Build a domestic tech ecosystem or stay dependent on adversaries who can cut you off at will? The answer's obvious. But building that ecosystem — fabs, quantum labs, AI infrastructure, biotech facilities — requires massive energy. You can't decarbonize and build industrial capacity simultaneously without crippling growth.

So China's sequenced it. Growth and tech independence first. Climate later, when the economy's strong enough and the tech stack's independent enough that cuts don't threaten sovereignty.

The carbon target isn't weak because China doesn't care about climate. It's weak because China decided tech sovereignty matters more right now. When you're racing the US for AI dominance and scrambling to replace banned semiconductors, you don't handicap yourself with emissions caps that slow production.

## The Numbers Tell the Story

7% annual R&D growth funds the future. 3-6% emissions growth pays for it. Both numbers come from the same plan because they're part of the same calculation: what does it cost to become technologically independent, and is China willing to pay that in carbon?

Answer: yes.

The West sees contradiction. China sees trade-offs. US chip bans made decoupling non-negotiable. Climate commitments became negotiable. The five-year plan makes that hierarchy explicit. You just have to read both sections.

This isn't climate denial. It's climate delay in service of industrial strategy. China's still installing record solar and wind — 240GW of solar in 2025 alone. But it's also building the factories, labs, and infrastructure to escape tech dependence. That second part costs more carbon than the first saves.

## What This Means

Western analysts cover China's tech push and climate targets separately. Tech: China's racing for AI supremacy. Climate: China's backsliding on commitments. Both true. Both incomplete.

Put them together: China decided it can't afford to lose the tech race and won't let climate targets slow it down. The plan allows emissions to grow because growth and independence come first. When you're building quantum computers and semiconductor fabs under export restrictions, you pay in carbon.

The 15th Five-Year Plan is Beijing's clearest statement yet on priorities. Tech self-sufficiency beats climate ambition. Growth beats cuts. Independence beats cooperation. Two stories, one document, unified strategy.

Nobody forced China to choose between tech and climate. US export controls did. The five-year plan is the answer.
